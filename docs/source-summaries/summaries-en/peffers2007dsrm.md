## Aim and development of DSRM

The paper provides a nominal process and presentation model for design-science research, complementing conceptual principles and quality guidelines. Its three objectives are consistency with prior literature, a usable nominal research process, and a mental model for communicating/evaluating design research. The methodology is developed by synthesising process elements from earlier IS and reference-discipline literature (Table 1, printed p. 53).

## Six activities and entry points

1. **Problem identification and motivation:** define the problem and justify the value of solving it.
2. **Objectives of a solution:** translate the problem and knowledge of feasible solutions into justified qualitative/quantitative goals.
3. **Design and development:** determine functionality and architecture and create the artefact, drawing on relevant knowledge.
4. **Demonstration:** use the artefact on one or more problem instances through an appropriate case, experiment, simulation, proof, or other activity.
5. **Evaluation:** compare observed results with the solution objectives using suitable evidence and metrics; iterate if needed.
6. **Communication:** explain the problem, artefact, novelty, utility, rigour, and evaluation to relevant audiences.

The nominal order is not an obligatory waterfall process. Research can start from a problem, desired objectives, an existing design/development idea, or a client/context-initiated demonstration. The researcher then addresses the necessary surrounding activities and can iterate (printed pp. 52–56; Figure 1).

## Demonstrations and evaluation evidence

The authors retrospectively interpret four already-published projects rather than running four prospective controlled trials of DSRM (pp. 57–73):

- **CATCH data warehouse:** supports health assessments and distributed data integration; evaluation includes manual checking of automatically generated reports.
- **Software-reuse measurement:** operationalises reusable development effort; application across five projects is compared with developers' independent relative assessments.
- **SIP voice/video application and middleware:** includes prototyping, debugging, technical performance and usability testing, and distribution to the Internet2 community.
- **Critical Success Chains at Digia:** combines structured interviews, preference maps, and an ideation workshop; validation is mainly proof-of-concept and organizational feedback.

These examples illustrate varied artefact types, entry points, and evidence. They support the framework's fit and explanatory usefulness. They do not prove that following six activities guarantees a valid method, a successful application, or superiority to other research approaches. The authors call for direct application in further research and discuss the relationship with action research and the difference between research and ordinary design practice.

## Relevance, boundaries, and citation use

**Primary:** §2.6 Research Methods and Evaluation. **Secondary:** §2.7 Synthesis and Research Gap. DSRM can organize the later development and assessment of TDRP and Masar. In the literature review, explain how related studies design and validate artefacts; reserve the project's actual protocol, participant plan, cases, and metrics for the methodology chapter.

The distinction between demonstration and evaluation is especially important. Running Masar on a worked example would demonstrate operation; independently checking criticality, MTPD/RTO, priority rules, and shared-dependency outputs would address specific correctness claims. Practitioner assessment of traceability/usefulness is additional evidence of a different kind. These are implications for this project, not measurements reported in the source. The paper supplies neither a business continuity algorithm nor empirical evidence about recovery outcomes, and it does not make DSRM the only legitimate research approach.
