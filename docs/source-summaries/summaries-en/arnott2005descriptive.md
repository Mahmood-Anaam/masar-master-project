# A Descriptive Analysis of Decision Support Systems Research Between 1990 and 2003

## Identity and reading basis

- **Authors:** David Arnott (Monash University), Graham Pervan (Curtin University of Technology), Gemma Dodson (Monash University).
- **Published:** *Australasian Journal of Information Systems*, 12(2), May 2005, pp. 178–191.
- **DOI / BibTeX:** https://doi.org/10.3127/ajis.v12i2.90, key `arnott2005descriptive`. One AIS eLibrary listing displays a different final DOI number (.91), so the identifier is flagged for re-checking.
- **Text examined:** the complete article, including all eleven tables and the reference list, through a full-text mirror (see the access record). Locators below are the journal's page numbers.

## Problem and contribution

The paper is the first major report of a long-running project that examines the theoretical foundations and professional relevance of decision support systems (DSS) research. It was motivated by three suspicions: that research was drifting away from practice, that the range of concepts addressed was narrow, and that DSS was dominated by a positivist, quantitative orthodoxy (p. 178). DSS is defined as the area of information systems concerned with supporting and improving managerial decision-making, spanning personal DSS, group support systems, executive information systems, online analytical processing, data warehousing, and business intelligence (p. 178). The study is a descriptive literature analysis. It reviews research; it does not propose or evaluate a DSS.

## Method

The project coded 926 DSS articles published in 14 journals between 1990 and 2003, divided into three periods (358, 397, and 171 articles; pp. 180–181, Table 1). The journals include Decision Support Systems (35.7% of the sample), MIS Quarterly, Management Science, Information Systems Research, and several European and group-decision journals. Articles were selected electronically by keyword and title and then checked manually against each issue's contents and the article text. A coding protocol was calibrated on example articles coded by two researchers, reviewed by a third, and then applied independently. Uncertain codes were adjudicated by one researcher, and the data were analysed in SPSS (p. 181). The three guiding questions concern research strategies and methods, decision-support focus and professional relevance, and judgement-theoretic foundations (pp. 179–180).

## Results actually reported

- **Paradigm and strategy (Tables 2–4):** among 608 empirical papers coded for paradigm, 91.8% were positivist and 8.1% interpretivist, against 81% positivist in a cited general IS study. About two thirds of the sample (67.1%) is empirical. Lab experiments account for 19.0%, descriptions of specific applications or systems 17.4%, surveys 7.3%, and case studies together 8.4% (positivist 5.2%, interpretivist 3.2%). By research stage, 65.6% is theory building, 26.2% theory testing, and 3.1% theory refinement.
- **DSS types (Table 5):** personal DSS 33.8%, group support systems 31.0%, intelligent DSS 13.7%, executive information systems (including business intelligence and OLAP) 7.5%, negotiation support 4.4%, knowledge-management-based DSS 1.8%, and data warehouses 1.2%. Personal and intelligent DSS fall across the periods (intelligent DSS from 16.2% to 8.2%), while negotiation support and data warehousing rise.
- **Focus (Table 6):** information technology 24.4%, system development 21.6%, decision-making process 19.5%, and decision outcome or organizational impact 15.9%. Attention to development and technology declines over time, while outcome and impact doubles.
- **Practical relevance (Table 7):** a subjective coding judgement. Only 9.5% of papers were rated high or very high, while 53.2% were rated low or none. The authors call this a potential crisis for the field (p. 185).
- **Clients and users (Table 8):** the primary client was unclear in 88.8% of papers and the primary user in 57.3%.
- **Decision-theory foundations (Tables 9–11):** 45.8% cite no judgement and decision-making research, 79.8% do not use a phase theorem of decision-making, and where decision theory is used, Simon's work dominates. Prescriptive approaches (50.1%) outnumber descriptive (24.3%), and behavioural approaches (37.0%) outnumber economic (21.2%). The paper reports seven summary conclusions (pp. 188–189).

## Critical reading and limits

1. **Dated and journal-bounded.** The sample ends in 2003 and consists of 14 journals. It does not include conference papers, books, or practitioner literature and cannot describe later developments such as cloud platforms, big data analytics, or machine learning.
2. **Subjective coding.** Practical relevance and "unclear" client coding rest on researcher judgement inferred from the article text. An "unclear client" means the paper did not state one, not that no stakeholder existed.
3. **Descriptive only.** Cross-tabulations and correlations are deferred to later project stages (p. 189), so no associations are claimed.
4. **Small reporting defects.** In Table 11 the count for "Both" in the total column reads 7.1 where 71 is implied by the other counts (926 minus 196, 343, and 316). Table 4 percentages are reported to one decimal place and should be quoted as printed.
5. **Not a design guide.** The paper evaluates a research field. It says nothing about rule-based, configurable, or business-continuity DSS and does not prescribe how to build or evaluate one.

## Use in the approved chapter

**Primary:** Section 2.5.1 Foundations. Use it for a citable definition and typology of DSS, the place of intelligent (AI-technique) and knowledge-management-based DSS in that typology, and a documented critique that DSS research is weakly grounded in decision theory and often fails to identify its clients and users. **Secondary:** Section 2.6 Research Methods and Evaluation (the prevalence of lab experiments and application descriptions, and the relevance concern as context for choosing evaluation designs) and Section 2.7 (a methodological justification for stating Masar's intended users and decisions explicitly).

Cite the numbers only as findings about 1990–2003 journal research. Do not place Masar in a specific DSS type from this paper alone; that classification is the author's own argument to make. Do not use the paper to claim that rule-based DSS are an established category, since its typology does not name them. Pair it with `power2007history`, which gives the historical narrative, and keep the two distinct: this paper supplies a research-field analysis, and Power supplies a historical account.
