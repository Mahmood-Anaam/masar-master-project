# The Application of Business Impact Analysis Due to Electricity Disruption in a Container Terminal

## Identity and reading basis

**Authors:** Eko Hariyadi Budiyanto, Raja Oloan Saut Gurning, Trika Pitana. **Publication:** *Sustainability*, 13(21), article 12038, 2021. **DOI:** https://doi.org/10.3390/su132112038. **BibTeX:** `container2021bia`. Complete 17-page publisher PDF examined; CC BY 4.0. Locators use PDF pages. Some figures contain duplicate text layers; these are extraction artefacts, not additional observations.

## Question and method

The paper adapts general BIA procedures to electricity disruption at an anonymised container terminal in Surabaya, Indonesia. Its unit of analysis is a terminal's operations and impact categories, not a portfolio of services to be ranked automatically. It combines qualitative identification of impacts and management-approved scales with quantitative calculations based on historical operational data before and after disruption (pp. 4–7).

Thirty commissioners, directors, and managers contribute questionnaires/interviews. Proposed impacts are selected through affirmative responses and reconciled with management. Focus-group discussion establishes contextual impact scales in light of objectives, business position, and risk preferences. Six categories are retained: loading/unloading productivity, economic loss, employee productivity, customer decline, reputation, and wasted energy. The scale has five levels from insignificant to catastrophic; it is not presented as a universally suitable organizational scale (pp. 7–9).

## Calculations and evidence

The worked disruption lasts 18 hours. Recorded operating time is 355.9 hours/month, nominal monthly time 730 hours, and corrective/preventive maintenance 0.83/9.09 hours. Calculated utilisation changes from 49.43% to 46.92%; the after/before ratio is 94.94%. This is **remaining relative productivity**, not a 94.94% loss (pp. 9–10).

The throughput-based economic proxy starts with 49,160 containers, ten cranes, and 17 containers/hour/crane. Eighteen lost hours correspond to 3,060 containers; remaining throughput is 93.78%. The paper uses throughput as a revenue proxy rather than reporting an audited monetary loss. For employees, 8 hours/day × 20 days gives 160 hours; subtracting 18 hours yields 88.75% remaining productivity, or an 11.25% reduction (pp. 10–12).

Customer change is represented by ship calls over a ten-day window; the reported decline is one ship and is classified as insignificant. Reputation is assessed by questionnaires and media context, with the largest deterioration described around the second day after disruption. For restart energy, the calculation is 12 kWh × ten cranes × four disruptions/year = 480 kWh/year (pp. 12–14). Distinguish this annualised estimate from the impact of a single 18-hour event.

## Findings and interpretation limits

Table 8 and the discussion classify economic impact as catastrophic; productivity, employee productivity, reputation, and wasted energy are moderate; customer decline is insignificant (pp. 14–15). However, the economic scale in Table 3 associates 94–96% remaining income with moderate impact and below 92% with catastrophic impact. The reported 93.78% result and its catastrophic label therefore need clarification. State the authors' reported classification and this inconsistency separately; do not treat the mapping as a validated computational rule.

The reputation text also mixes a decline *to* approximately 50% with wording about a decline *by* 50%. The graph and narrative should be checked before quoting a precise percentage change. Some cited standard designations contain editorial errors; verify standards against their own records rather than copying those labels.

The study supports industry-specific criteria and the value of combining operational records with stakeholder judgement. Its evidence is one terminal and one selected disruption context. It does not provide an independent causal comparison, a reusable ranking algorithm, computed MTPD/RTO values for multiple services, or an evaluated software system. Sector-specific criteria and short observation windows limit generalisation; data are available from the corresponding author on request.

## Use in the literature review

**Primary:** §2.2.1 BIA Process and Impact Categories. **Secondary:** §§2.2.2, 2.2.4, 2.6, 2.7. It provides concrete examples for configurable impact dimensions, context-dependent scales, and the separation of raw measurements from impact classifications. It also motivates consistency checks between a documented scale and calculated outputs. Do not present it as direct validation of TDRP's temporal threshold derivation, highest-criticality inheritance, or recovery sequencing.
