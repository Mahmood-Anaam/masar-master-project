## Problem and framework

FEDS addresses how to choose an evaluation strategy, not simply how to list possible evaluation methods. It links why, when, how, and what to evaluate. Two independent dimensions classify evaluation episodes: **formative versus summative purpose**, and **artificial versus naturalistic setting/paradigm** (pp. 78–82).

Formative evaluation informs improvement; summative evaluation judges outcomes against expectations. Neither is synonymous with timing. Ex-ante/ex-post distinguish when evaluation happens; either can serve different purposes. Artificial evaluation may use controlled experiments, simulation, formal argument, or proof. Naturalistic evaluation examines real users, a real system, and real problems/settings. Each has trade-offs: control and repeatability do not automatically establish real-use effectiveness; realism introduces confounding and cost.

## Strategies and design process

The four prototype strategies are Quick & Simple; Human Risk & Effectiveness; Technical Risk & Efficacy; and Purely Technical Artefact. Selection depends on design risks, the cost/feasibility of real-user evaluation, the nature of the artefact, and evaluation goals. They may be combined and revised; the diagram's number and placement of episodes are illustrative, not a prescribed sample size (Figure 2; Table 1).

The four-step process is to explicate goals, choose strategy/strategies, determine properties to evaluate, and design individual evaluation episodes (pp. 82–84). Relevant goals include rigour, uncertainty reduction, ethics, and efficient use of resources. Properties must be appropriate to the artefact and its situation. The examples include correctness, reliability, usability, efficiency, flexibility, comprehensibility, and organizational outcomes. The article's historical quality-standard examples are not a requirement to use an obsolete quality standard in this project.

## Demonstration and validation

Two earlier studies illustrate contrasting evaluation choices: the GIST website-development/management model and a workflow-oriented information distribution design. The authors use FEDS to explain their evidence and suggest stronger evaluation, without assuming unreported activities occurred (p. 85).

A naturalistic summative assessment examines use of FEDS in a doctoral project developing a Site-Storming method for site-specific games. Three design iterations combine artificial/formative work with later, more naturalistic/summative episodes, including conceptual game design, real projects, workshops, and comparison. The paper uses that case as evidence that FEDS helps select and adapt a research evaluation strategy (pp. 85–88). The authors explicitly acknowledge the small number of studies and recommend more applications, varied artefacts, hybrid strategies, and training materials. This is methodological evidence, not a measured effect on continuity outcomes.

## Implications for the review and future evaluation

**Primary:** §2.6 Research Methods and Evaluation. **Secondary:** §2.7. FEDS supports distinguishing evaluation of TDRP's rule logic from evaluation of Masar's implementation and practitioner interpretation. Controlled cases can test deterministic output properties; practitioner tasks can examine usefulness and explanation. Such a combination would be a project-specific application, not an evaluation already performed.

Do not label a synthetic demonstration “naturalistic” merely because its example resembles a real organization. Conversely, interviews alone do not establish computational correctness. Define the evaluated property, reference criterion, setting, participants/data, and inference limit for each episode. Neither the four strategies nor the four steps prescribe TDRP's thresholds, a participant count, or a claim of improved live-incident recovery.
