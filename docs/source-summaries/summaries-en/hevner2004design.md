## Purpose and conceptual framework

The paper positions design science and behavioural science as complementary approaches in IS. Behavioural research explains/predicts phenomena; design research builds and evaluates purposeful artefacts addressing relevant organizational problems. An artefact can be a construct, model, method, or instantiation. An implemented prototype demonstrates feasibility, but implementation alone does not establish utility or a research contribution (pp. 75–84).

The research framework links an **environment** of people, organizations, and technology with **IS research** and a **knowledge base** of foundations/methodologies. Business needs establish relevance; applying and extending prior knowledge establishes rigour. Building and evaluating form an iterative loop. The distinction between routine development and design research depends on a clear knowledge contribution, not merely on using a new application stack (Figure 2; pp. 79–82).

## Seven guidelines and evaluation choices

The guidelines require a viable artefact; an important problem; rigorous evaluation; clear, verifiable contributions; rigorous construction/evaluation methods; design as a search process; and effective communication to technical and managerial audiences (Table 1, p. 83). They are adaptable considerations, not a mandatory mechanical sequence.

Evaluation should match the artefact, its purpose, and appropriate metrics. The method catalogue includes observation through cases/field studies; analytical static, architectural, optimisation, and dynamic analysis; controlled experiments and simulation; functional/structural testing; and descriptive informed arguments/scenarios (Table 2, p. 86). Listed properties include completeness, consistency, accuracy, functionality, performance, reliability, usability, and organizational fit. Scenario demonstrations or attractive interfaces alone therefore cannot substantiate every possible quality claim.

Search is described as generating and testing alternatives under goals and environmental constraints. Satisfactory solutions may be appropriate when optimal search is infeasible. Contribution may concern an artefact, design foundations, or evaluation methodologies. Formalism can improve precision but can also remove relevant organizational conditions if applied uncritically (pp. 87–90).

## Evidence and limits

Three published exemplars illustrate the guidelines: anonymity mechanisms in group DSS; an exchangeable workflow language and architecture; and TOP Modeler for emergent knowledge processes (pp. 90–97). They show different evidence types, including formal proofs/cost analysis, implemented prototypes, and iterative organizational use. These are illustrative analyses, not a controlled comparison establishing that the guidelines improve all research projects. The paper acknowledges challenges in representation, theory, evaluation, and generalisation from individual projects (pp. 98–100).

## Implications for TDRP and Masar

**Primary:** §2.6 Research Methods and Evaluation. **Secondary:** §§2.5.1, 2.7. TDRP can be described as a method/model contribution and Masar as its instantiation; this classification is an application of the framework, not the paper's own finding about this project. The review should establish prior knowledge and the bounded contribution before the methodology chapter operationalises construction and evaluation.

For later evaluation, distinguish rule correctness, software conformance, and practitioner usefulness. Expected outputs independently derived from declared rules can test implementation; stakeholder interpretation can assess a different property. This paper does not prescribe TDRP's algorithm, validate its thresholds, establish standards compliance, or prove that a prototype improves real incident outcomes. Avoid treating the seven guidelines as six DSRM activities: that process model is addressed separately by Peffers et al.
