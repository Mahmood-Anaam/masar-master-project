# Business Continuity Management—Identifying Relevant Processes for a Reference Model

## Identity and reading coverage
Daniel Arias-Aranda, Knut Haufe, Srdan Dzombeta, Vladimir Stantchev. Applied Sciences 16(7), article 3219, 26 March 2026. DOI: https://doi.org/10.3390/app16073219. Citation key: `bcms2026reference`. Complete 21-page publisher PDF obtained; main text, tables, declarations and reference list examined. CC BY 4.0. No separate process-flow diagrams are supplied: the article reserves their elaboration for future publications.

## Question, context and construction method
The study asks which processes belong in a BCMS process reference model. It addresses the operational gap between implementing standards as a checklist and running a sustained management system, rather than proposing a service-ranking algorithm. ISO 22301:2019 is the reference for matching processes against ISO 22300:2025, ISO 22313:2020 and ISO/TS 22317:2021 (§§2–3). A seven-stage comparison selects standards and a reference, identifies processes, fixes abstraction level, develops profiles, matches models and reports results. The profile template covers purpose, category, inputs, outputs, activities, example metrics and references. Strong/partial/weak/no relationships form a qualitative mapping scale (Table 1); these are analyst classifications, not measured effect sizes.

Core-process criteria are regularity, input–output transformation, operational execution, BCM-manager ownership/management, and direct stakeholder value (Figure 4). Initial analysis treats initial BCMS planning as a project, governance as management, four other processes as support, and remaining processes as core. These classification decisions are distinct from whether an activity is important.

## Empirical verification and findings
The authors approached 47 German professionals; 39 responded: 14 continuity managers (10 private-sector, four public-sector) and 25 consultants. Respondents classified a predefined list and could add processes; no additions were proposed. At least 80% recognition meant approval, 20–80% discussion, and below 20% dismissal (§4.2). BIA/criticality analysis, incident handling, recovery, exercises, and resource management each received 39/39 recognition. Requirements management and strategy selection each received 29/39 (74%); implementation management and plan/procedure development each received 27/39 (69%). Thus expert consensus was uneven even for important activities.

The authors retained several disputed processes by returning to standards and argument, rather than treating the consensus threshold as an automatic inclusion rule. Table 5 consolidates **22 processes**: one management, 17 core and four support, with initial planning discussed separately. It connects requirements → BIA/criticality → risk assessment → strategies/solutions → plans/implementation, and includes exercises, competence, suppliers, audit, performance, change and improvement. BIA produces approved continuity requirements with rationale, not an executable resource-constrained restoration schedule (§4.3).

A public-sector pilot reportedly showed integration synergies with information security and better operational focus, but required contextual adaptation and differing maturity levels (§5). No controlled comparator, quantified efficiency gain, live-incident recovery effect or reproducible pilot dataset is provided. Proposed fuzzy-inference decision support is future work (§6), not an implemented/evaluated capability.

## Critical assessment and use in this thesis
The standards mapping and practitioner review offer complementary conceptual and face-validity evidence. Limits include one-country professional sampling, a consultant-heavy panel, predefined-list anchoring, author judgement in disputed classifications and limited pilot reporting. There are internal classification tensions: §4.2 discusses consultation/communication as core, but Table 5 assigns it support; the claim that all approved processes were initially core also conflicts with earlier treatment of governance and resources. The rough PDCA–disaster-cycle analogy should not be treated as a universally established equivalence.

Use chiefly in §2.1 BCM and §2.6 methods; cross-reference §2.2 and §2.7. It situates TDRP/Masar inside the BIA/criticality decision function of a wider BCMS and supports documenting ownership, inputs, outputs and review. It does **not** validate TDRP thresholds, prove Saudi applicability, establish ISO certification, or show that existing BCM tools lack configurable prioritization.
