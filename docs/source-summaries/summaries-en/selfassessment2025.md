# An Approach to Business Continuity Self-Assessment

## Identity and reading coverage
Nelson Russo, Henrique São Mamede, Leonilde Reis. Technologies 13(6), article 242, 11 June 2025. DOI: https://doi.org/10.3390/technologies13060242. Key: `selfassessment2025`. Complete 37-page publisher PDF obtained, CC BY 4.0; main text, scoring tables, figure explanations, declarations and references examined. Screen images illustrate the tool; this review has not obtained its editable questionnaire/software or participant-level dataset.

## Problem and artifact
The artifact evaluates organizational BCM preparedness and guides improvement investments. It responds to fragmented assessment instruments and difficulty translating standards into practical checks. Its FAMMOCN foundation has eight components and 118 activities; the rapid self-assessment selects **61 questions**, not 118 questions. Components are top-management commitment, understanding the organization, managing risk, consolidating continuity strategy, planning/structuring response, implementing/maintaining plans, checking the BCMS, and improving it (§§5.1–5.4, Table 3).

Questions have an identifier, component/activity affiliation, question weight, domain, question text, interpretive guidance, scorecard and notes/evidence (Table 1). Questions and activities have nested weights, then component weights contribute to a total score. Each level's percentage weights sum to 100; calculations need normalized fractions even though the schematic tables print products without explicit percentage divisors. A reproducible implementation should state that normalization rather than copy the display literally. Question, component and total outputs lie on a 0–100 scale. Red is below 60, yellow 60 to below 90, green 90–100 (§5.3). A later green interval prints an open upper endpoint; the explicit accepted-score definition includes 100. These colors are diagnostic indicators rather than validated, rigid maturity classes.

The authors report deriving content and weights from an earlier 167-publication review and expert input, including a five-expert review/Delphi process and focus-group weighting (§3). These are their reported derivation claims: this review has not independently reconstructed their prior review, weighting dataset or all 61 item texts. Figures 6–14 illustrate component questions and automatic scoring, with **random example values**; illustrated totals are not observed organizational performance.

## Research and evaluation
The paper follows a problem-centered, six-activity design-science process with iteration, demonstration and evaluation (§3). Its five-person focus group comprises senior practitioners in ICT governance, security, risk and public-sector IT, with 16–27 years of experience. A 90-minute session includes a simulated financial-institution cyberattack and reflection on participants' organizations (§6). Findings informed score guidance, notes fields, documentation and crisis-communication support.

Semi-structured interviews involved ten organizations: 30% medium-sized and 70% large, across sectors including finance, insurance and critical infrastructure (§7). Participants received the tool beforehand and discussed their interpretation and responses. A delivery-services example describes resource-allocation and leadership-awareness benefits. The article's **50% recovery-time reduction is an expectation**, not an independently measured outcome. Reported 100% validation of a feature is a local feedback claim; it does not establish universal validity or ISO certification. The paper also reports 80% organizational improvement on risk-assessment scoring within six months and 60% discovering underestimated supply-chain exposure, but supplies no participant-level trajectories or raw dataset permitting independent verification.

## Critical appraisal
Evaluation mainly supports perceived usefulness, content relevance and refinement. It lacks a controlled comparison or demonstrated causal reduction of real disruptions. Although SMEs motivate the work, the evaluated sample contains medium and large organizations, limiting transfer to micro-enterprises. Weighting and self-reports remain context-sensitive, and higher preparedness scores need external corroboration.

Reporting needs caution: the five-person focus group is associated with a 70% agreement figure (§6.2), which cannot be a simple whole-person proportion of five; denominators/aggregation are not explained. Claims of six-month progression coexist with the statement that full longitudinal case studies were not conducted (§8). The ethics declarations state “not applicable” despite the narrative's informed-consent account. These tensions should be reported, not repaired with invented details.

## Use for Masar
Primary mapping: §2.5 continuity DSS and §2.6 evaluation; secondary §2.1 and §2.7. It is a useful worked example of configurable questions, weighted rules, explanations, visual reporting and practitioner evaluation. Its ranking target is BCM improvement/readiness, whereas TDRP targets service criticality and recovery priority from temporal impacts. A preparedness score must not be relabeled MTPD, RTO, service criticality or recovery-order validation. It supports separating calculation verification, user interpretation and organizational outcome claims.
