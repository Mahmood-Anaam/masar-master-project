## Purpose and organizing framework
This author-written retrospective organizes DSS history around communication-, data-, document-, knowledge- and model-driven systems. It combines published accounts with recollections and explicitly invites correction; it is neither a systematic literature review nor a new experiment. Its contribution here is conceptual orientation. The historical treatment of other authors is secondary evidence: cite Power for this synthesis, and obtain originals before attributing detailed findings directly to those original studies.

Sections II–III describe early interactive modeling, decision support for less-structured management problems, behavioral and design frameworks, and the database–model–dialog architecture. DSS categories overlap; classification describes a dominant decision-support mechanism rather than a universally exclusive partition. Historical reports and databases alone do not automatically provide interactive decision support.

## Five categories and web delivery
Model-driven DSS emphasize manipulation of quantitative, financial, optimization or simulation models; they can use small parameter sets rather than large databases (§IV.1). Data-driven systems emphasize retrieval/manipulation of organizational data, including data warehouses, OLAP and executive information systems (§IV.2). Communications-driven systems emphasize collaboration, conferencing and group decision processes (§IV.3). Document-driven systems retrieve and analyze text/documents, with search as a central support tool (§IV.4). Knowledge-driven systems apply specialized domain knowledge to suggest actions; historical examples include rule-based expert systems (§IV.5).

The web is a delivery platform across these categories, not a sixth exclusive inference mechanism (§V). The article describes browser-delivered tools and information hosted on a server, intranets, hosted services and integrated portals. It traces their expansion from the mid-1990s. Its references to particular browsers and technologies are historical examples, not contemporary implementation recommendations.

## Evidence and limitations
The taxonomy and historical narrative are useful for explaining what a DSS supports. The article does not establish that any category always improves decisions, measure BCM performance, validate deterministic impact rules, or prescribe service-recovery parameters. Its old forecasts should not be repeated as current observed trends. The version mismatch and character-encoding artifacts in the HTML warrant restrained use of exact wording; no quotations are needed.

## Application in the thesis
Use chiefly in §2.5.1 DSS Foundations, with a connection to §2.5.3 tools. It helps distinguish data storage, rule calculation, explanations and human decision authority. Masar can be discussed as combining structured data with explicit analytical/rule support, but that classification is our project interpretation. Calling TDRP rule-based does not imply machine learning, autonomous decisions, full expert-system equivalence, or proven effectiveness. Architecture, explainability and calculation correctness need their own project evidence.
