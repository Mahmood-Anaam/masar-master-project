\label{ch:literature-review}

\section{Purpose and Review Approach}
\label{sec:review-approach}

Business continuity decisions require more than a description of important services. They require an account of how interruption affects those services, when consequences become unacceptable, and what must be available for recovery. The literature approaches these questions through management frameworks, business impact analysis (BIA), maturity assessment, information systems, process mining, and restoration optimization. These approaches address related problems at different levels. This chapter examines their relationships and limitations to establish a defensible foundation for Masar and its proposed Time-Dependent Recovery Prioritization (TDRP) method.

The review proceeds from general business continuity management (BCM) concepts to the methods and systems closest to the project. It asks three connected questions. First, what information is needed to relate business consequences to recovery requirements? Second, how do existing methods represent criticality, dependencies, and recovery priorities, and what evidence supports their results? Third, which design and evaluation requirements follow for a bounded information system that will provide traceable decision support? The purpose is to synthesize evidence for an applied IS project, without treating the existence of a technical artifact as evidence of organizational effectiveness.

A structured thematic review was conducted using public scholarly web searches, DOI metadata, publisher sites, and institutional repositories, with searches completed on 16 October 2026. Query families combined business continuity or business impact analysis with recovery objectives, prioritization, dependencies, decision support, information systems, process mining, maturity, and evaluation. References in retrieved papers guided additional searches. Crossref metadata and publication records were checked against the downloaded texts. This was a purposive review based on full texts; it was not an exhaustive systematic database search, and no search of subscription Scopus or Web of Science collections is claimed.

The analytical corpus contains fourteen complete academic articles and two NIST guidance documents. The academic articles cover conceptual distinctions, BCM processes, empirical organizational studies, impact measurement, prioritization methods, software-supported assessment, and design science evaluation. Full academic texts, including their reported methods, results, limitations, and appendices, were read before synthesis. The updated NIST BIA report was read in full. The longer contingency planning guide was consulted through its BIA, recovery-priority, testing, maintenance, and template sections. Guidance is used to clarify concepts and information requirements, separately from empirical evidence about a method's effectiveness.

Selection required relevance to the review questions, an identifiable DOI, and accessible full text for substantive comparison. Older contributions were retained when they provided a distinct analytical approach; recent work extends coverage to process mining, self-assessment, restoration scheduling, and process reference models. Abstract-only records were not treated as fully reviewed studies. The repository records source locations, reading scope, and critical notes for each included text. This procedure makes the synthesis inspectable while acknowledging that full-text availability and the English-language focus may omit relevant approaches. Consequently, the gap established here is bounded to the reviewed evidence.

\section{Conceptual Foundations of Business Continuity}
\label{sec:bcm-foundations}

\subsection{Continuity, Risk, and Resilience}

BCM concerns an organization's arrangements for sustaining and recovering the activities needed to deliver its products or services. Its object is broader than restoring a server or recovering a database. A functioning application may support an unavailable business service if personnel, facilities, information, or external providers remain unavailable. Conversely, a service may continue temporarily through a manual alternative even when its normal application is inaccessible. This distinction between technical restoration and business continuity is reflected in NIST's separation of business processes, system resources, and recovery priorities \cite{swanson2010contingency}.

Galaitsi et al. examine the relationship among business continuity, operational resilience, and organizational resilience through distinctions concerning risk, assets and processes, and normal and crisis conditions \cite{galaitsi2023distinctions}. Their conceptual analysis helps explain why apparently similar continuity initiatives may pursue different objectives. A process-focused intervention might preserve an existing service, whereas an organization-focused response might change the way value is delivered. Their distinctions are an analytical proposal rather than universally settled definitions. The illustrative organizational cases support interpretation, but they do not establish a measured causal relationship between adopting a framework and improved resilience.

For Masar, this conceptual work establishes a boundary on the contribution that can reasonably be claimed. A repository and prioritization method can support the interpretation of recovery information. They cannot, by themselves, demonstrate that the organization can adapt to every disruption or maintain all stakeholder outcomes. Resilience depends partly on capabilities and decisions outside the proposed application. Accordingly, the artifact will be evaluated against specific information and decision tasks, rather than against an unrestricted claim to make an organization resilient.

Risk analysis and BIA also answer different questions. A risk assessment considers potential events, their likelihood, vulnerabilities, and consequences. BIA examines the consequences associated with the loss or degradation of activities and resources. The updated NIST guidance explicitly distinguishes determining business effects from estimating the probability of a risk scenario \cite{quinn2025bia}. An activity can therefore require rapid recovery because its interruption would be unacceptable, even when a particular initiating event is considered unlikely. Combining likelihood and recovery urgency into one unexplained score would conceal this distinction.

\subsection{The Management System and Its Information Flows}

A business continuity management system (BCMS) includes responsibilities, policies, analysis, strategies, plans, exercises, and improvement processes. It is a management system, which may be supported by software. Arias-Aranda et al. propose a reference model comprising twenty-two BCM processes organized into management, core, and support processes \cite{arias2026processes}. Their standards mapping and expert consultation make explicit the relationships among process inputs, outputs, and responsibilities. This perspective is useful for IS design because continuity information acquires meaning through the activity that creates, approves, or uses it.

The study invited forty-seven German specialists and obtained thirty-nine responses. An 80\% agreement threshold informed the analysis, but some processes below that threshold were retained on the basis of the authors' standards interpretation \cite{arias2026processes}. The final model therefore combines expert judgment and reasoned synthesis. It should not be represented as a set of processes mechanically validated by unanimous agreement. The reported pilot also supports initial application rather than a controlled estimate of performance improvement. Its geographical and organizational context limits direct transfer to a Saudi organization without local requirements work.

The process model and NIST guidance converge on an important point: continuity information must move between organizational levels and activities. Business owners describe consequences; technical teams assess supporting resources; responsible managers determine acceptable requirements; subsequent exercises provide evidence about capability \cite{arias2026processes,swanson2010contingency}. These activities require related records, but they should not overwrite one another. An assessed limit, an approved target, and an observed restoration time represent different kinds of information. Maintaining their relationships is a more precise integration objective than placing all continuity documents in one location.

Standards alignment in the reviewed studies is treated with similar care. Arias-Aranda et al. and Russo et al. interpret several standards and frameworks in constructing their models \cite{arias2026processes,russo2022maturity}. This chapter uses those interpretations as reported research findings; it does not claim direct verification of every underlying ISO clause. Nor does implementing the proposed functions establish certification. Masar will support a selected set of continuity tasks within one organization, while the broader governance system remains responsible for policy, assurance, and organizational adoption.

\section{Organizational Evidence and Continuity Practice}
\label{sec:organizational-evidence}

\subsection{Resources Required for Continuity}

P\u{a}unescu et al. investigate BIA practices in 119 Romanian small and medium-sized enterprises using structured interviews and regression analysis \cite{paunescu2018bia}. Their resource categories include people, premises, technology and communication, information, and suppliers. In the adjusted model, supplier and partner identification, expertise, alternative premises, communication arrangements, and information have significant positive associations with the BIA measure. These findings broaden the information that a continuity application should represent. Concentrating exclusively on application inventories would omit resources that respondents associated with their continuity analysis.

The evidence has limits that affect its use in design. The dependent BIA measure incorporates critical-resource considerations, so it is conceptually close to several predictors. Respondents report organizational practices rather than observed recovery outcomes. The local sample and one respondent per enterprise further constrain generalization \cite{paunescu2018bia}. Some predictors, including IT in the adjusted model, are not individually significant. This does not mean that IT is unnecessary for continuity. It means that the study does not establish an independent adjusted association for every resource category under its measurement and sample conditions.

Masar can draw an information requirement from the breadth of these categories without importing regression coefficients as priority weights. A supporting resource should have a type, an owner, and a defined relationship to the service it enables. External providers require similar attention, because their capability and commitments cannot be inferred from an internal service owner's assessment. The study supports looking beyond technical assets; it does not prescribe universal weights for people, applications, facilities, or suppliers.

\subsection{From Analysis to Strategy and Response}

P\u{a}unescu and Argatu examine relationships among BCM arrangements, BIA, risk assessment, strategy, response planning, exercising, and culture \cite{paunescu2020functions}. Risk assessment is significant in their adjusted strategy model, whereas BIA and scope arrangements are not. Strategy is associated with response planning, and response planning, exercising and review, and culture are associated with reported BCM effectiveness. These results direct attention to the translation of analysis into organizational action. Producing a BIA document is insufficient evidence that its conclusions have been incorporated into an approved recovery strategy.

The reported effectiveness model explains 81.9\% of variance, but that value requires careful interpretation. Effectiveness is operationalized as a composite drawn from the same broad domains used as predictors \cite{paunescu2020functions}. Strong model fit therefore does not demonstrate an equivalent improvement in actual recovery performance. It also does not establish that a software intervention causes better continuity. For the present project, the useful implication is to test the connection between records and decisions directly, rather than relying on a broad self-reported effectiveness score.

The 2018 and 2020 Romanian studies report the same final sample size, collection period, and respondent profile \cite{paunescu2018bia,paunescu2020functions}. They should therefore be treated as related evidence, without assuming independent samples. Their initial questionnaire or interview totals also differ. This does not invalidate their distinct analytical questions, but it prevents counting them as two independent replications of the same finding. A literature comparison that counts publications alone would overstate the diversity of the underlying organizational evidence.

Labus et al. provide a different setting through an adaptive e-business continuity framework implemented in three Serbian financial organizations \cite{labus2020adaptive}. Thirty-eight key users evaluated components covering BIA, risk assessment, continuity planning, and continual improvement. BIA and continual improvement have significant direct associations with the adaptive e-BCM construct in the reported PLS-SEM analysis. The direct relationship between BIA and continuity planning is unsupported, prompting the authors to propose a more explicit recovery-objectives parameter. That adjustment is a subsequent design response, rather than a separately evaluated result.

The small pooled sample, self-reported measures, and common sector limit causal and cross-sector conclusions. Nevertheless, the study offers a particularly relevant design lesson: recovery objectives need an explicit place between impact analysis and planning \cite{labus2020adaptive}. Masar will therefore preserve links from an impact assessment to a candidate requirement, its approval, and the associated recovery procedure. This addresses a relationship that the study itself identifies as requiring clarification, while leaving the effectiveness of the proposed information structure open to evaluation.

\subsection{Adaptation and Implementation Constraints}

Fani and Subriadi examine the use of a continuity planning framework across banking, electricity, water services, and manufacturing cases \cite{fani2019multiusable}. Their analysis identifies differences in how framework activities were performed and proposes revisions, including changes to the ordering of impact analysis and priority determination and explicit budget consideration. Training, testing, and periodic review were not consistently completed under organizational policies and time constraints. The cases show why implementation should be examined at the activity level rather than reduced to a binary statement that a framework was adopted.

An important evidential distinction concerns the revised framework. The observed cases concern application of the earlier framework; the resulting revision is not itself subjected to an equivalent new evaluation in that article \cite{fani2019multiusable}. Masar should follow the same distinction when reporting its development. Practitioner suggestions may justify changing a rule or workflow, but the revised artifact then requires verification. Feedback supporting a change cannot automatically serve as evidence that the completed change has improved task performance.

Taken together, these organizational studies favor a modest and adaptable scope. They support recording resources, responsibilities, planning links, and review requirements, while showing that organizational commitment and available effort affect implementation \cite{labus2020adaptive,fani2019multiusable}. A configurable application must therefore balance local relevance with the effort required to maintain it. Every additional field or indicator should have an identifiable decision purpose. Otherwise, the system risks reproducing the burden of a complex framework without strengthening the reasoning behind a recovery priority.

\section{Business Impact Analysis and Recovery Requirements}
\label{sec:bia-requirements}

\subsection{Impact Dimensions and Organizational Context}

BIA relates interruption to consequences that matter to the organization's mission. NIST's updated report describes impact in terms of mission, finance, reputation, and the criticality and sensitivity of assets; it also addresses confidentiality and integrity losses alongside availability \cite{quinn2025bia}. This is broader than the interruption-focused TDRP proposal. The report supports consistent assessment categories and shared asset information, but it does not require Masar to implement a complete enterprise cybersecurity risk programme. Defining that boundary prevents the project from treating all forms of business risk as interchangeable recovery inputs.

Budiyanto et al. illustrate the contextual nature of impact measurement in a container-terminal case \cite{budiyanto2021bia}. Their analysis considers an eighteen-hour disruption using dimensions including productivity, economic impact, employee productivity, customer effects, reputation, and energy consumption. Thirty management respondents and focused discussion inform the assessment. Operational indicators are translated into consequences for the terminal. The study demonstrates how domain knowledge can make a generic BIA more concrete, but its proxies and thresholds depend on the particular operation and available records.

Several implications follow from the way these measures are constructed. Throughput can inform an economic proxy without being identical to realized financial loss; ship calls can inform customer consequences without directly measuring every customer's experience. The distinction between a percentage remaining and a percentage lost must also be explicit. Moreover, the paper's economic classification contains an apparent mismatch between a stated category range and the reported case classification \cite{budiyanto2021bia}. This cautions against copying published category tables without checking their boundary behavior and correspondence with the results.

For Masar, impact dimensions should therefore include definitions, units where applicable, and the meaning of each severity category. A financial assessment and an operational assessment need not use identical evidence. The system should preserve the underlying rationale instead of presenting heterogeneous categories as a precise common currency. A configurable matrix can support consistency within an organization, but configuration requires review. The terminal case does not establish a universal severity scale or demonstrate that a given downtime duration has the same significance across services.

\subsection{Time-Dependent Impacts and Discrete Observations}

NIST's contingency planning guide connects supported processes, outage impacts, estimated tolerable downtime, resource requirements, and recovery priorities \cite{swanson2010contingency}. Its worked template also requires the drivers behind timing objectives. These elements support the use of time-dependent assessments, but they do not supply a universal mathematical transformation from severity categories to an exact recovery time. Labus et al. similarly include disruption timescales and business impacts as framework parameters \cite{labus2020adaptive}. Timing is thus an established continuity concern, rather than an independent novelty claim for Masar.

A useful distinction is between the elapsed duration of an interruption and the time at which that interruption begins. Consequences may change with duration, but they can also depend on deadlines, peak workloads, or the availability of alternatives. The updated NIST report recognizes timing and partial-loss scenarios in impact analysis \cite{quinn2025bia}. Masar's initial scope will represent assessed consequences across elapsed-time intervals within a documented context. It will not predict the progress of a live incident. Assessments made for a peak operating period should retain that assumption when compared with assessments for normal demand.

Discrete observations require a further qualification. Consider an illustrative assessment in which consequences are acceptable at four hours and unacceptable at eight hours. Those observations do not prove that the transition occurs exactly at eight hours. Under an explicit assumption that the relevant consequence does not decrease during that interval, the crossing lies after the earlier observation and no later than the later one. Without that assumption or adequate intermediate evidence, the system should report only what was observed. This example explains the proposed treatment of assessment uncertainty; it is not a result taken from a reviewed experiment.

The implication for TDRP is to preserve the distinction between an earliest observed unacceptable interval and an exact business tolerance limit. Interpolation between ordinal categories would add precision that the observations do not justify. A threshold not reached within the assessment horizon also requires a qualified result: the available assessment has not established the limit. Missing observations, inconsistent profiles, and unapproved thresholds should create review conditions. None should silently become evidence of low impact or unlimited tolerance.

\subsection{Tolerance, Targets, and Demonstrated Capability}

NIST distinguishes maximum tolerable downtime for a business process, a recovery time objective (RTO) for system resources, and a recovery point objective (RPO) for data \cite{swanson2010contingency}. The reviewed papers use related terms, including maximum acceptable outage and maximum tolerable period of disruption. Masar will use the terminology defined in the introductory chapter, while retaining the scope of each source's measure. Differences in terminology should not obscure the more important distinction between how long the business can tolerate interruption and how quickly a particular resource is intended to be restored.

An RTO must leave adequate opportunity for the supported service to resume before consequences become unacceptable. Restoring a database may be followed by reconciliation, backlog processing, validation, and business resumption. NIST explicitly notes that additional process work affects the relationship between the resource RTO and tolerable downtime \cite{swanson2010contingency}. Accordingly, choosing an RTO as a fixed fraction of a tolerance limit would require independent justification. A numerically smaller target can still be infeasible when the required people, data, and technical capabilities are unavailable.

RPO concerns the acceptable recovery point for data; it is not another duration to add to an interruption limit. The minimum business continuity objective (MBCO) concerns an acceptable level of service during disruption, which may be below normal capacity. These requirements should be retained when supplied by responsible stakeholders, but interruption severity alone cannot establish acceptable data loss or minimum processing capacity. The resource and process distinctions in NIST's guide support keeping such requirements separately identifiable \cite{swanson2010contingency}.

Finally, a requirement is not proof of capability. A target may be proposed, approved, or supported by a dated exercise; each state carries a different evidential meaning. NIST recommends documented action when an RTO cannot be met and emphasizes testing, training, exercises, and maintenance \cite{swanson2010contingency}. Masar's proposal adopts this separation by linking requirements to capability evidence and exceptions where available. The system will support review of these relationships, while the actual adequacy of a recovery arrangement remains a matter for organizational testing and approval.

\section{Methods for Prioritization and Dependency Analysis}
\label{sec:prioritization-methods}

\subsection{Structured Estimation and Business Continuity Points}

Podaras et al. propose Business Continuity Testing Points (BCTP), adapting concepts from use-case-based estimation to recovery analysis \cite{podaras2016tools}. The method considers actors, business functions and processes, technical and environmental factors, and unexpected recovery conditions. Weighting and ranking procedures organize these inputs into a structured estimate. Its value lies in making assumptions and contributing factors visible, particularly where an organization lacks extensive historical recovery data. It provides a useful comparison for a rule-based method because both approaches depend on explicit input definitions and documented transformations.

The illustrative results also reveal a distinction that matters to Masar. Higher complexity or criticality is mapped to shorter recovery time estimates in the examples \cite{podaras2016tools}. Such a mapping can express a desired urgency of recovery, but it does not independently demonstrate the time that recovery work will actually require. A complex activity can be urgent and difficult to restore at the same time. Interpreting urgency as a capability estimate would collapse two decisions that continuity planning needs to keep separate.

The article provides illustrative calculations and discusses agreement with recovery categories, while acknowledging the need for further organizational testing \cite{podaras2016tools}. These results support examination of the method's structure rather than adoption of its numerical factors as universally validated parameters. Masar can retain the advantages of inspectable rules while explicitly identifying whether an output is a business requirement, a relative priority, or an estimate supported by recovery evidence. A common output label should not conceal differences in how those quantities were produced.

\subsection{Association Mining for Continuity Information}

Idrees et al. propose a mining-based business continuity information system for educational institutes \cite{idrees2019bcis}. Their work combines questionnaire-derived information, statistical analysis, and association mining using Apriori. The reported case concerns an Egyptian university faculty. A continuity information system structure relates organizational information to BIA and planning concerns, while mined associations are intended to support continuity knowledge. The study is relevant because it already brings together continuity concepts, information organization, and analytical processing. Integration alone is therefore insufficient to distinguish Masar.

The paper reports 17,683 transformed records, 27,373 rules, and successive filtering that yields ninety-three selected rules at a confidence threshold of at least 80\% \cite{idrees2019bcis}. These record counts should not be interpreted as numbers of independent organizational participants. More fundamentally, association-rule confidence describes co-occurrence conditional on an antecedent in the analysed data. It is not the predictive accuracy of a recovery-time estimate, a probability that a continuity plan will succeed, or evidence that implementing a rule causes better recovery.

Several statistical statements and numerical interpretations in the paper are difficult to reconcile, which further limits reliance on its precise reported effects \cite{idrees2019bcis}. The appropriate comparison is therefore between analytical purposes and evidential requirements. Association mining can reveal patterns worth investigating when suitable data exist. Masar instead proposes explicitly configured decision rules that can be reproduced without a training dataset. That choice fits the present scope, but it still requires validation of rule meaning, handling of exceptions, and usefulness to the intended users.

The proposed information system also includes recovery and continuity concepts that overlap with Masar, such as timing objectives, minimum operating requirements, and dependencies \cite{idrees2019bcis}. Masar's potential contribution must consequently be evaluated through the specific chain linking time-indexed evidence, qualified requirement candidates, approvals, and priority explanations. It cannot rest on an assertion that earlier continuity information systems omit all of these concepts. The difference sought is in the inspectable organization and evaluation of that chain within a bounded artifact.

\subsection{Process Mining and Impact Propagation}

Raptaki et al. develop Process Risk Impact Assessment (PRIA), implemented through Java and ProM, to propagate cybersecurity impacts across business processes \cite{raptaki2025impact}. Event logs support a weighted process graph, allowing effects originating at a point of interest to be propagated through connected activities. The case uses enterprise procurement records from a Greek transport company. The reported log contains 553,763 events associated with 110,275 purchase orders, and the evaluation includes a ransomware case and an additional hypothetical interruption scenario. This provides a stronger data-grounded representation of process relationships than an entirely illustrative dependency list.

The work is especially close to Masar in its concern with indirect consequences. A service's impact cannot always be understood from its own local record because interruption can affect downstream activities. PRIA demonstrates an approach for examining propagation and addresses loops in process behavior \cite{raptaki2025impact}. Expert interviews and comparisons with alternative propagation configurations provide evidence about the model and its application. These features make it a substantive comparator, rather than simply a general cybersecurity background reference.

Nevertheless, the dependency and measurement semantics differ from the present proposal. PRIA's propagated impact is relative to its graph, weights, initial conditions, and threshold. It is not automatically a monetary loss, an MTPD, or an approved RTO. Event logs also represent recorded activity; missing manual work and unrecorded dependencies remain outside that observation unless supplied separately. Asset mapping and interpretation still require organizational knowledge \cite{raptaki2025impact}. A large event log therefore improves the evidence for observed process behavior without guaranteeing a complete continuity model.

Masar will use documented service and resource dependencies because comprehensive event logs cannot be presumed for its initial implementation. This is a scope and data-access choice, rather than a claim that manual dependency entry is inherently more accurate. The proposed system should identify dependency provenance and review status, while acknowledging that an incomplete map can produce incomplete warnings. PRIA suggests a possible later extension involving process-derived evidence, but automatic discovery and calibrated impact propagation remain outside the current TDRP commitment.

\subsection{Restoration Optimization and Operational Feasibility}

Shahverdi et al. address recovery through stochastic optimization of interdependent lifelines, including power, water, and roads, with priority given to critical societal services \cite{shahverdi2025recovery}. Their model updates decisions as crews obtain information, combines restoration and inspection choices, and permits decision-maker intervention. Computational methods include progressive hedging, scenario reduction, and parallel processing. This work addresses an operational scheduling problem with explicit decisions and constraints, whereas a relative priority list expresses urgency without determining a feasible allocation of resources.

In an illustrative regional network with 182 lifeline elements and two hospitals, prioritizing hospital service restores the hospitals in eleven rather than thirteen time increments in a reported comparison, while overall completion remains fifty-seven increments \cite{shahverdi2025recovery}. This is evidence under the model's case and assumptions. It should not be generalized as a guaranteed reduction in every organization's recovery time. The study's significance is the explicit representation of trade-offs and dependencies, which allows a proposed schedule to be examined against its objective and resource conditions.

The same explicitness reveals its limits. Network elements are modeled as functioning or not functioning; repair takes one known time increment in the base model; and hospitals become functional when supporting lifelines are restored. Partial restoration, uncertain repair duration, and more detailed service-resumption work require extensions \cite{shahverdi2025recovery}. Those assumptions are reasonable modeling choices for the stated analysis, but they limit transfer to an organizational service that needs staff mobilization, data reconciliation, or staged capacity recovery.

Masar will therefore expose the distinction between urgency and precedence. A high-priority service may require a shared resource that supports other services, while two independent high-priority services may compete for the same recovery team. Dependency visibility can identify the first kind of issue. Resolving the second requires durations, resource availability, and a scheduling objective beyond the present scope. Masar's output will remain a reviewable priority presentation with prerequisites and exceptions, without claiming the feasibility guarantees of a restoration optimization model.

\section{Maturity Assessment and Governance Support}
\label{sec:assessment-systems}

Russo et al. propose FAMMOCN, a framework for the multidisciplinary assessment of organizational maturity in continuity \cite{russo2022maturity}. Its components comprise a BCM model, measurement system, self-assessment system, and implementation guide. Eight BCM areas connect management commitment, understanding the organization, risk management, strategy, planning, implementation, checking, and improvement. The measurement structure records weights, responsibilities, frequencies, goals, and sources of evidence. It therefore demonstrates that useful continuity software support can include the governance of information, rather than concentrating only on calculating priorities.

The framework was developed through design science and evaluated using a five-person expert focus group and professionals from ten medium-sized and large organizations \cite{russo2022maturity}. The organizational evaluation involved advance self-assessment, simulated use, and semi-structured interviews. It replaced a fuller case-study evaluation under access and confidentiality constraints. Positive participant judgments provide evidence about perceived usefulness, clarity, and applicability. They do not establish that the framework reduces incident downtime, and the difficulty of recruiting smaller organizations limits claims about its effectiveness for that group.

A useful tension appears in the framework's development. Experts valued transparent weights and concise assessment, while warning that too many metrics and excessive parameterization could create additional work \cite{russo2022maturity}. This matters to Masar's proposed shared repository. Traceability requires sufficient information to understand a result, but maintaining unused data imposes costs on service owners. The literature supports retaining ownership, evidence, and review attributes where they serve an identifiable purpose. It does not justify adding an extensive maturity assessment merely because a dashboard can display it.

The later self-assessment study extends the same research programme with a model of eight components and 118 activities, using sixty-one selected questions to produce weighted scores \cite{russo2025self}. The tool provides 0--100 assessment values and visual indicators, with red below sixty, yellow from sixty to below ninety, and green from ninety. The questions and supporting instructions help participants interpret the assessment and identify improvement areas. These scores describe reported preparedness relative to the model; their cutoffs should not be repurposed as service-impact thresholds or recovery-time rules.

The later evaluation again uses expert interaction and interviews with ten organizations, primarily medium-sized and large enterprises \cite{russo2025self}. Its illustrated scorecards use demonstration values, and a delivery-sector case describes an expected 50\% reduction in recovery time. The expectation is not an observed recovery result. Some reported percentages and longitudinal statements lack sufficient detail to reconcile their denominators or evaluation timing. These reporting limitations favor cautious use of the study's strongest evidence: a specified assessment structure and documented user feedback, rather than a causal estimate of recovery improvement.

The two FAMMOCN publications should also be understood as related development, without assuming that they represent independent evaluations across twenty organizations \cite{russo2022maturity,russo2025self}. Their methodological contribution to Masar lies in iterative refinement, evidence attributes, and the need to explain assessment values. Their functional difference lies in the unit of analysis. Organizational maturity concerns the establishment and management of continuity practices, while TDRP concerns the interpretation of specific service requirements and priorities. A mature organization can still have an infeasible service RTO; a low overall maturity score does not by itself determine which service must recover first.

\section{Research Methods and Implications for Evaluation}
\label{sec:review-evaluation}

The corpus uses several research approaches that suit different questions. Organizational surveys investigate relationships among practices, but their cross-sectional results and self-reported constructs limit causal inference \cite{paunescu2018bia,paunescu2020functions,labus2020adaptive}. Case-based framework studies reveal implementation conditions and adaptation needs \cite{fani2019multiusable}. Process-log analysis and optimization can examine formal behavior under explicit data and assumptions \cite{raptaki2025impact,shahverdi2025recovery}. Masar should draw on these strengths without assuming that a single questionnaire or a set of calculated examples can support every type of claim.

Design science is particularly relevant because the project proposes a method embodied in an information system. FAMMOCN demonstrates how expert feedback can inform an artifact and how evaluation constraints affect the resulting evidence \cite{russo2022maturity}. However, the reviewed studies also show why demonstration and evaluation need separate descriptions. Demonstrating that a prototype produces a result establishes that a task can be performed under the demonstration conditions. Evaluation must additionally assess whether the result meets defined expectations and whether the artifact assists its intended users.

Venable et al.'s Framework for Evaluation in Design Science (FEDS) distinguishes formative from summative purposes and artificial from naturalistic settings \cite{venable2016feds}. These dimensions are useful because technical correctness and practical usefulness require different evidence. A synthetic case can isolate a threshold boundary or dependency conflict, while practitioner tasks can examine whether users understand the resulting explanation. Involving practitioners does not make a hypothetical exercise equivalent to field deployment during a real disruption. The setting and the scope of inference should remain explicit.

For IS597, an initial evaluation episode should examine rule consistency through documented cases with independently specified expected outcomes. Cases should cover observations exactly at a threshold, a crossing between assessed intervals, missing observations, a threshold not reached, equal priorities, and incompatible target relationships. Dependency cases should include chains, shared resources, an absent prerequisite, and a cycle. Correctness concerns conformity to the approved specification; it does not mean that the specification itself is appropriate for every organization. Practitioner review of the specification is therefore a complementary activity.

A later episode should examine traceability and interpretation. Users should be able to identify the assessment version, threshold definition, rule configuration, approval state, and dependency evidence behind a result. A correct number accompanied by an incorrect explanation would fail this requirement. Similarly, a warning that users cannot interpret may provide little task value. Task outcomes should therefore include correctness of the user's conclusion and ability to identify unresolved conditions, alongside retrieval or completion time. These measures align the evaluation with the information problem defined in the introductory chapter.

Where access permits, comparison with a manual workflow should provide equivalent information and cases. Otherwise, differences may reflect better source data rather than the contribution of the artifact. Task order, prior experience, and learning effects should be considered when interpreting performance. Sample size and recruitment constraints should be reported honestly, with descriptive results and qualitative explanations appropriate to the available evidence. This evaluation plan remains prospective: no participants have been recruited and no implementation results are reported in the present IS595 chapter.

\section{Research Gap and Design Implications for Masar}
\label{sec:review-gap}

The gap identified by this review concerns the inspectable relationship between several established continuity activities. The reviewed evidence does not establish, in one evaluated artifact matching Masar's bounded scope, the complete connection from discrete time-indexed impact observations to qualified recovery-requirement candidates, approved targets, dependency-aware relative priorities, and explanations that retain assessment and rule provenance. Individual parts are already addressed by prior research. The opportunity is to design and evaluate their explicit connection for defined continuity tasks, rather than to claim the invention of BIA, dependencies, or rule-based prioritization.

This positioning distinguishes three related contributions. The information contribution concerns shared service and resource identities, assessment versions, approval states, and links to procedures and capability evidence. The method contribution concerns explicit treatment of threshold observations, candidate requirements, ties, missing information, and prerequisite conflicts. The presentation contribution concerns making the result and its supporting evidence accessible to service owners, continuity coordinators, and technical recovery personnel. Their value must be assessed together, because a reproducible rule with inaccessible evidence may still fail the intended task.

Several choices remain for detailed requirements and method specification. The organization must determine impact dimensions, unacceptable-consequence criteria, assessment horizons, and approval responsibilities. TDRP must define how relative priorities are ordered, how equal cases are presented, and how exceptions affect eligibility for comparison. These are substantive design decisions, not formatting details. The review supplies their rationale and constraints without pretending that one published threshold or weight set is universally applicable. Configuration changes must retain a version so that recalculated results remain explainable.

The expected contribution is consequently an applied design contribution whose usefulness can be tested. Demonstrating reproducibility and traceability would support claims about the artifact's behavior. Showing more accurate interpretation or reduced retrieval effort in equivalent tasks would support bounded claims about user assistance. Neither result alone would show reduced real-world disruption losses. Establishing such effects would require additional longitudinal and operational evidence beyond the present project's proposed evaluation. This boundary is consistent with the distinctions between evaluated properties and evaluation settings in FEDS \cite{venable2016feds}.

\section{Review Limitations and Chapter Summary}
\label{sec:review-summary}

The review is limited by purposive selection, public full-text availability, and the contexts represented in the selected studies. The corpus includes financial institutions, Romanian SMEs, a container terminal, an educational institution, transport procurement, and modeled critical infrastructure. These settings provide useful contrasts but do not establish requirements for a specific Saudi organization. No local prevalence estimate for fragmented continuity information is inferred from them. Local requirements elicitation and practitioner review will be necessary before the proposed implementation can be judged against its intended environment.

Publication-level reporting creates further uncertainty. Some studies contain numerical inconsistencies, overlapping constructs, unclear evaluation denominators, or anticipated benefits presented near empirical findings. Related publications can also draw on the same sample or research programme. The synthesis addresses these issues by distinguishing reported results, author interpretations, and the present project's design inferences. It does not pool effect sizes or treat expert agreement as a common measure of effectiveness. The supplied source notes preserve these qualifications for subsequent review and detailed design.

The literature establishes that continuity decision support must connect business consequences, recovery requirements, supporting resources, and organizational responsibilities. Existing work contributes management-process models, contextual BIA, structured estimates, mined associations, propagated process impacts, maturity tools, and constrained restoration methods. Their different outputs and evidence boundaries explain why integration alone is an insufficient contribution claim. Masar is positioned around the traceable interpretation of time-indexed impacts and recovery priorities, with explicit uncertainty, approval, and dependency information. This foundation supports the subsequent IS595 discussion of the proposed solution and anticipated applications, while implementation and evaluation remain the work of IS597.
