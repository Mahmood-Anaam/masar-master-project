\chapter{Literature Review}
\label{ch:literaturereview}

This chapter examines the literature relevant to business continuity and critical service recovery prioritization, with particular attention to the relationship between time-dependent impact assessments, recovery requirements, and explicit decision rules. The review brings together continuity standards and national guidance, business impact analysis research, prioritization methods, relevant dependency models, and decision support systems. It moves from the general field toward the specific problem defined in Chapter~\ref{ch:introduction}, and concludes by assessing the evidence for the proposed Time-Dependent Recovery Prioritization (TDRP) methodology and its implementation in Masar.

The chapter is organized from the general to the specific. Section~\ref{sec:lr_bcm} presents the concepts, international standards, and Saudi national guidance on which business continuity management rests. Section~\ref{sec:lr_bia} narrows the focus to business impact analysis, time-dependent impact assessment, and the derivation of recovery parameters. Section~\ref{sec:lr_prioritization} reviews approaches to service criticality and recovery prioritization, and Section~\ref{sec:lr_dependencies} examines supporting dependencies and the assignment of their criticality. Section~\ref{sec:lr_dss} examines decision support concepts and comparable continuity management systems. Section~\ref{sec:lr_methods} reviews the research and evaluation methods applied in related studies. Section~\ref{sec:lr_gap} synthesizes the reviewed evidence, establishes the supported research gap, and positions the project against it, and Section~\ref{sec:lr_summary} summarizes the chapter.

\section{Business Continuity Management}
\label{sec:lr_bcm}

The vocabulary of continuity work is not used uniformly in the literature, and several of its central terms overlap. A systematic review of business continuity research observes that the definition of business continuity differs from one research object to another, and it traces the field back to the disaster recovery practices of the 1980s \cite{russo2023maturity}. The contingency planning guide of the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) separates business continuity plans from continuity of operations plans, crisis communications plans, cyber incident response plans, disaster recovery plans, and occupant emergency plans, although its own scope is limited to restoring information systems and leaves business-process continuity outside \cite{nist2010contingency}. From an optimization viewpoint, Sahebjamnia et al. argue that the distinction matters in practice: continuity planning and recovery planning draw on the same scarce resources, and treating them as independent conceals that competition. They present the integration of the two as a route toward organizational resilience \cite{sahebjamnia2015integrated}.

In this thesis, business continuity refers to the capability of an organization to keep delivering its prioritized services at an acceptable level during and after a disruption. Business continuity management (BCM) denotes the management activity through which that capability is established and maintained, and a business continuity management system (BCMS) is the management system through which an organization carries out that activity, which is the object of ISO 22301 \cite{iso22301catalogue}. Resilience is the broader term, and the literature often presents continuity as one of its components. Disaster recovery and emergency response are treated here as parts of, or inputs to, continuity management. Risk management is related but not identical. The National Framework of the Saudi National Risk Council places BCM inside a resilience cycle that runs from anticipation and assessment through prevention, continuity, preparedness, response, and recovery \cite{nrcnationalframework}, and NIST guidance on using business impact analysis (BIA) to inform risk prioritization states that the BIA does not directly address identified risks, although the criticality it determines feeds their prioritization \cite{quinn2025bia}.

The remainder of this section reviews the documents that define the field in practice. Section~\ref{sec:lr_standards} compares international standards and guidance, and Section~\ref{sec:lr_saudi} examines the Saudi national documents that form the applied setting of the project.

\subsection{Standards and Frameworks}
\label{sec:lr_standards}

This subsection compares four documents that together cover a requirements standard, guidance on its implementation, guidance on impact analysis, and a government guide for information systems (Table~\ref{tab:lr_standards}). The treatment of the three ISO documents rests on their published scope descriptions and not on a clause-level reading, since the full texts are commercial publications.

\input{tables/lr_standards_comparison}

ISO 22301:2019 is the requirements standard for a BCMS, and ISO 22313:2020 offers guidance on applying it across organizations of different types, with the aim of sustaining delivery at a predefined acceptable capacity \cite{iso22301catalogue,iso22313catalogue}. The business impact analysis has its own technical specification, ISO/TS 22317:2021, which describes a documented analysis adapted to the needs, objectives, resources, and constraints of the organization and which does not impose one uniform process \cite{iso22317catalogue}. This indicates that the form of the analysis, and therefore the way assessments are turned into recovery targets, is left for each organization to settle.

The NIST guide is more concrete, although its subject is narrower. It organizes contingency planning into seven steps, one of which is the BIA, and it divides the analysis into three tasks: identifying business processes and their recovery criticality, identifying the resources they depend on, and setting recovery priorities for those resources \cite{nist2010contingency}. It defines maximum tolerable downtime (MTD) as the total outage a process can accept, and the recovery time objective (RTO) as the unavailability permitted for a system resource supporting that process. The RTO should normally be shorter than the MTD, because work that follows restoration, such as reprocessing, consumes part of the tolerable period. The recovery point objective (RPO) concerns the tolerated loss of data and is not added to the MTD as a duration \cite{nist2010contingency}. The guide also notes that restoration has technical prerequisites, for example that an operating system be restored and verified before the applications and data that run on it. Section~\ref{sec:lr_sequencing} returns to this point, because it shows that a ranking of importance does not by itself amount to a recovery schedule. The times and cost figures in the guide are illustrative, and it states no threshold that organizations are expected to adopt. The Saudi documents discussed in Section~\ref{sec:lr_saudi} use the term maximum tolerable period of disruption (MTPD) for what corresponds, in practice, to the MTD of the NIST guide.

Two recent studies examine how such frameworks fit together. Russo et al. compare ISO 22301:2019, CMMI v2.0, COBIT 2019, ITIL 4, and NFPA 1600 against common BCM components and find that these frameworks describe how, when, and by whom measurement takes place but leave the organization to decide what to measure \cite{russo2023maturity}. Arias-Aranda et al. matched processes across ISO 22301, ISO 22313, ISO/TS 22317, and ISO 22300 to build a reference model of 22 BCMS processes, and 39 German practitioners (14 continuity managers and 25 consultants) took part in verifying the list \cite{bcms2026reference}. All 39 recognized BIA and criticality analysis as a process of the system, as they did incident handling, recovery, exercises, and resource management, whereas agreement on some other processes was lower. The same study positions the BIA as the source of approved continuity requirements with their rationale, not as a means of producing an executable restoration schedule \cite{bcms2026reference}. Its evidence is better read as support for the face validity of the process list than as proof of its adequacy, since the panel came from one country, consultants made up most of the respondents, and respondents classified a predefined list.

Taken together, these documents agree that the BIA is a central step, that it should yield time-based recovery parameters, and that it must be adapted to the organization. In the material reviewed here, they do not prescribe how impact assessments made at several points in time are converted into an MTPD or an RTO, nor how the priority of a service is carried over to the resources that support it. NIST supplies definitions, relationships between parameters, and templates, and the ISO documents state scope and adaptability, but neither supplies a rule for the conversion. The Saudi documents reviewed next go further by fixing impact tiers and time targets, although they too leave the conversion itself open.

\subsection{National Guidance in Saudi Arabia}
\label{sec:lr_saudi}

Two national documents are most relevant to the setting of the project. The first is the National Framework for Risk, Emergency and Business Continuity Management issued by the General Secretariat of the National Risk Council, of which the business continuity chapter was examined (the extract carries no publication year). The second is the Guideline for Risk and Business Continuity Management for Digital Government of the Digital Government Authority (DGA), reviewed in its Arabic version (3.0) and its English issue (1.0), both dated January 2025 \cite{dga2025bcm,dga2025guidelineen}.

The National Framework treats BCM as an integrated management capability for essential services, necessary products, and the resources that enable them, and it describes a planning phase that covers policy, scope, governance, roles, and performance indicators \cite{nrcnationalframework}. Its treatment of the BIA is the part most relevant here. The organization may choose a suitable approach and collect data through interviews, workshops, and questionnaires, following a sequence that runs from identifying services and products, through the processes that enable them, to impact criteria, impacts over time, resumption timeframes, priorities, resources, and dependencies, and ends with an approved consolidated report. The suggested impact criteria include financial, legal and regulatory, reputational, beneficiary, operational, health and safety, and contractual consequences, and the time points follow the scale chosen by the entity. The outputs are the MTPD, the RTO, the minimum business continuity objective (MBCO), and, for data-dependent activities, the RPO. The framework offers an illustrative classification of recovery times, ranging from the most critical class of zero to eight hours to a lowest class of two weeks, and it presents these values as examples and not as compulsory thresholds \cite{nrcnationalframework}. It also distinguishes one-way from mutual dependency and upstream, internal, and downstream relationships, and it groups dependencies as physical, cyber or digital, geographic, and logical. These dependencies are inputs to the BIA and to the assessment of resources.

The DGA guideline presents itself as general guidance that does not replace the requirements of standards, and it organizes the system as a Plan--Do--Check--Act cycle \cite{dga2025guidelineen}. Its BIA section asks the entity to inventory its operations and procedures together with their dependencies, to evaluate the consequences of disruption over time using an impact matrix approved by the entity, and to record recovery requirements. The outputs include the RTO, the RPO, the MTPD, the MBCO, and the required people, equipment, systems, and facilities. The point at which the impact timeline starts depends on the sensitivity of the service, and the guideline gives a half-hour interval and a starting point measured in days as examples of this variation. Its element of most direct interest here is a matrix that classifies each platform or application into five levels, using stakeholder, financial, legal and regulatory, and reputational impact. Where the factors point to different levels, the highest one is adopted, and the five levels are associated with targeted recovery times of 4, 8, 12, 24, and 48 hours, which remain subject to continuous review \cite{dga2025guidelineen}. Among the planning mistakes the guideline lists are missing system priorities and missing dependency maps.

Read together, the two documents are explicit about the elements of the analysis and about the parameters it should yield, but they leave a good deal to the entity. Both ask the entity to define its own impact criteria and time scale. The DGA matrix does document a rule that precedes the work of this thesis, namely selection of the highest impact level and a mapping from tier to recovery time. It assigns one level and one recovery time to each platform, however, and it does not describe how assessments made at successive points in time are converted into an MTPD or an RTO. In the passages examined, neither document specifies how dependencies should inherit the criticality of the services they support, only that dependencies should be mapped and considered. These are the gaps that make a configurable and traceable rule set a reasonable object of study, and Section~\ref{sec:lr_bia} turns to the way the research literature treats the analysis that precedes it.

\section{Business Impact Analysis}
\label{sec:lr_bia}

The business impact analysis (BIA) supplies the service-level information on which the rest of a continuity program depends, and it is the stage whose outputs the proposed methodology takes as its input. Section~\ref{sec:lr_bia_process} considers the unit of assessment, the collection of impact data, and the impact categories used. Section~\ref{sec:lr_time_impact} looks at how studies represent the growth of impact with the duration of an interruption, Section~\ref{sec:lr_parameters} discusses the recovery parameters that such assessments are meant to yield, and Section~\ref{sec:lr_bia_approaches} compares the analytical approaches proposed in the literature. The definitions of the main parameters were given in Section~\ref{sec:lr_standards}, and the use of impact assessments for ranking services is left to Section~\ref{sec:lr_prioritization}.

\subsection{BIA Process and Impact Categories}
\label{sec:lr_bia_process}

The documents reviewed in Section~\ref{sec:lr_standards} describe the BIA as a structured assessment of what an interruption would cost an organization and how quickly those costs become unacceptable. The studies that apply it differ first in the unit of assessment. The NIST guide works from business processes and the system resources that support them \cite{nist2010contingency}, whereas the Saudi documents assess services or platforms \cite{nrcnationalframework,dga2025guidelineen}. Applied studies choose units that are finer or coarser still. Horalek assessed seven dataset groups and eleven system and asset entries of an automated meter management environment \cite{amm2023bia}, while Budiyanto et al.\ assessed the operations of one container terminal as a whole \cite{container2021bia}. NIST IR 8286D starts from the essential products and processes of the enterprise, identifies the assets that enable them, and records the resulting impact values and their rationale in a BIA register \cite{quinn2025bia}. The unit matters because every later quantity, whether a tolerable period or a priority, is attached to it.

Impact data are in most cases elicited rather than measured. Horalek combined public technical material with repeated guided interviews with dataset sponsors and owners, and the paper reports neither the number of respondents nor any measure of agreement between them \cite{amm2023bia}. Budiyanto et al.\ questioned thirty commissioners, directors, and managers, retained the impacts that respondents affirmed, and reconciled them with management; a focus group then fixed the impact scales in light of the terminal's objectives, business position, and risk preferences \cite{container2021bia}. That study is distinctive in supplementing judgement with operational records. Productivity, throughput, and working hours before and after a disruption were converted into remaining-capacity ratios, which were then mapped to a five-level scale running from insignificant to catastrophic \cite{container2021bia}.

The impact categories are correspondingly dependent on context. The terminal study retained six categories, namely loading and unloading productivity, economic loss, employee productivity, customer decline, reputation, and wasted energy \cite{container2021bia}. The metering study assessed legal and contractual obligations, organizational operation, essential service provision, confidence and reputation, and financial loss, alongside a classification of the data by confidentiality, integrity, and availability \cite{amm2023bia}. NIST IR 8286D presents mission, financial, and reputational consequences as illustrative domains to be adapted, and it separates criticality (the consequence of losing availability) from sensitivity (the consequence of losing confidentiality or integrity) \cite{quinn2025bia}. The common pattern is that categories and scales are set by the organization, which is also the latitude the National Framework leaves in presenting its own classes as illustrative (Section~\ref{sec:lr_saudi}).

Documenting a scale does not guarantee that classifications follow it. In the terminal study the economic impact of the eighteen-hour outage is reported as catastrophic, yet the scale presented in the paper assigns remaining income of 94 to 96 percent to the moderate level and values below 92 percent to the catastrophic level, so the calculated figure of 93.78 percent of remaining throughput does not map unambiguously to the reported class \cite{container2021bia}. The metering study uses a four-grade scale in one table and a five-point scale in the text that introduces its main matrix \cite{amm2023bia}. These discrepancies show why a method intended for repeated use needs explicit and checkable mappings between measurements, scale levels, and outputs.

\subsection{Time-Dependent Impact Assessment}
\label{sec:lr_time_impact}

Time enters the BIA through the question of how the impact of an interruption changes as it continues. The national documents treat the time scale as a matter for the entity, in the sense described in Section~\ref{sec:lr_saudi}, and the research literature offers a wider range of treatments.

The most explicit time-indexed assessment among the studies reviewed is the metering case. Horalek assessed availability at seven durations (30 minutes, 2 hours, 12 hours, 24 hours, 2 days, 1 week, and more than a week) against the same impact criteria, so that every asset received a row of ratings that either worsened with duration or did not \cite{amm2023bia}. The maximum tolerable period of disruption was then read as the time band in which high impacts arise, and a separate medium-impact period was defined for the point at which medium impacts begin \cite{amm2023bia}. The reported values range from twelve hours for the accounting system to more than one week for the customer system \cite{amm2023bia}. The paper also records that the same outage can have different consequences depending on its position relative to billing or reporting deadlines, so that duration alone does not describe timing \cite{amm2023bia}. One reported pair, a twelve-hour period for high impact together with a two-day period for medium impact in the accounting row, appears difficult to reconcile with the stated meaning of the two periods \cite{amm2023bia}. It illustrates how easily the ordering between thresholds can be violated when a matrix is completed by hand.

Other studies treat time differently. The terminal study examines one disruption of eighteen hours and compares operating records before and after it, and it describes the deterioration of reputation as greatest around the second day, which indicates that dimensions of impact need not follow the same time profile \cite{container2021bia}. Von der Assen et al.\ model persistent impact as a daily loss reduced by a recovery level over stated time frames, for example a total outage lasting four days, and treat the maximum tolerable period as an input and not a result \cite{vonderassen2024quanttm}. Optimization studies represent time through trajectories of operating level. Sahebjamnia et al.\ define the loss of resilience through the depth and duration of degraded operation, require operations to reach a minimum acceptable level after the tolerability deadline, and impose non-decreasing recovery trajectories \cite{sahebjamnia2015integrated}. Aghabegloo et al.\ likewise compute loss and recovery time from an illustrative degradation and recovery geometry \cite{assetcriticality2023}. In both cases the temporal profile is a modeling device inside an optimization or criticality calculation, not an assessment of impact thresholds for a service.

Guidance from NIST points to the same concern in qualitative terms. NIST IR 8179 asks how long an adverse operating state must persist before it becomes severe \cite{nist2018criticality}, and NIST IR 8286D recommends considering the timing and duration of an event and the possibility of partial loss of function \cite{quinn2025bia}. Commercial documentation describes comparable tables in which impact ratings are assigned to successive durations, and at least one platform applies a configured maximum when no rating reaches the intolerable level \cite{servicenow2025rto}; these tools are examined in Section~\ref{sec:lr_tools}.

Taken together, the studies show that time-indexed impact matrices, and the reading of a tolerable period from them, are established practice. They differ in granularity, from a handful of durations to continuous trajectories, and in whether the dimensions of impact are read separately or combined. Among the sources examined, few say how a threshold time is determined when impacts are observed only at discrete points, how a missing assessment at one period is handled, or what happens when a rating equals the threshold. The case in which no assessed period reaches the unacceptable level is addressed only in the documentation of a commercial platform \cite{servicenow2025rto}. These are the points at which an explicit interpretation rule is needed, and they are taken up again in Section~\ref{sec:lr_gap}.

\subsection{Recovery Parameters}
\label{sec:lr_parameters}

Three notions are easily conflated: the limit of what the organization can tolerate, the target it sets for recovery, and what it has demonstrated it can achieve. The maximum tolerable period of disruption belongs to the first, the recovery time objective to the second, and the results of exercises and incidents to the third. Section~\ref{sec:lr_standards} gave the definitions of the NIST guide, and Section~\ref{sec:lr_saudi} showed that the Saudi documents place the RTO inside the tolerable period.

The relationship between them is stated most explicitly in the metering study, which requires the RTO not to exceed the medium-impact period or the MTPD and the recovery point objective not to exceed the maximum tolerable data loss \cite{amm2023bia}. In that study the tolerability limits are read from the temporal matrix and the RTO is then chosen subject to these constraints, so that the limit is derived from the assessment while the target remains a decision \cite{amm2023bia}. The DGA guideline instead assigns the RTO from the tier of the highest-rated impact factor (Section~\ref{sec:lr_saudi}), and one commercial platform computes it as the minimum of the downtimes found for the individual impact categories \cite{servicenow2025rto}. Sahebjamnia et al.\ take the MTPD and a minimum business continuity objective from the BIA as inputs, and the model then determines recovery trajectories and times; the recovery times reported in their case sometimes exceed individual MTPD values and are discussed as exceptions \cite{sahebjamnia2015integrated}. This is a reminder that a tolerability limit and the recovery that a plan can actually deliver are different quantities.

The third notion, demonstrated capability, is the least developed. The National Framework lists the comparison of actual with target recovery times among its indicative performance indicators, but as a management measure and not as an observed result \cite{nrcnationalframework}, and the metering study does not quantify whether its proposed controls would achieve the stated recovery times in real incidents \cite{amm2023bia}. The separation is clear in definition but rarely reflected in reported evidence.

Terminology also varies. Sahebjamnia et al.\ use the recovery point objective for the operating level at which operations resume, which differs from its usual meaning as a limit on data loss \cite{sahebjamnia2015integrated}; this thesis keeps the usual meaning.

Figure~\ref{fig:lr_temporal_parameters} illustrates, with invented values, how the three notions relate when impact is assessed at discrete points. Because the assessment gives ratings only at the marked points, the time at which the unacceptable level is first reached lies somewhere between the last acceptable point and the first unacceptable one. The metering study reads this as a band, which avoids a spurious precision but does not settle which single value should be adopted as the tolerable period \cite{amm2023bia}. None of the sources reviewed states a rule for choosing within the band.

\subsection{Analytical Approaches to BIA}
\label{sec:lr_bia_approaches}

The analytical literature on the BIA can be grouped by what a method is meant to estimate. Matrix-based approaches rate impacts on ordinal scales and read recovery requirements from them, as in the metering study \cite{amm2023bia}. Data-assisted approaches calibrate the scales against operating records \cite{container2021bia}. Financial approaches convert scenarios into expected loss, as the preprint of von der Assen et al.\ does by multiplying the probabilities of initiation and success by the loss estimated over the recovery stages \cite{vonderassen2024quanttm}. Optimization approaches take tolerability limits from the BIA and decide how scarce resources are allocated to operating levels over time \cite{sahebjamnia2015integrated}. Criticality-index approaches rank assets by combining weighted sustainability and resilience factors in a fuzzy and probabilistic model \cite{assetcriticality2023}. Process-mining approaches infer dependencies between activities from event logs and propagate the relative impact of an incident through them \cite{raptaki2025propagation}. The data demands of the quantitative approaches are considerable. The model of Sahebjamnia et al.\ needs importance weights, resource requirements at each operating level, disruption likelihoods, and budgets for a case with four products, ten operating levels, and five disruption types \cite{sahebjamnia2015integrated}. The criticality framework of Aghabegloo et al.\ builds a Bayesian network from expert elicitation, with a structure that depends on thresholds chosen by the authors \cite{assetcriticality2023}, and the process-mining approach needs event logs, here more than half a million events from one procurement process \cite{raptaki2025propagation}. The matrix-based and data-assisted approaches ask less of the organization but depend on judgement and on the quality of the scales, as the inconsistencies noted in Section~\ref{sec:lr_bia_process} show.

The outputs are not interchangeable. Only the matrix-based studies yield tolerable periods and recovery targets directly \cite{amm2023bia}; the optimization study consumes the MTPD as an input \cite{sahebjamnia2015integrated}, and the other approaches produce a loss, a score, or a ranking that must still be associated with the underlying assets \cite{vonderassen2024quanttm,assetcriticality2023,raptaki2025propagation}.

The evidence of effectiveness is of one kind. The studies rely on single cases with expert review or hypothetical disruptions: the metering study is a worked assessment without a comparison of recovery outcomes, the model of Sahebjamnia et al.\ is evaluated under an additional hypothetical disruption, the framework of Aghabegloo et al.\ on face validity and sensitivity analysis, and the process-mining and financial approaches on interviews with five employees and a focus group of five participants, respectively \cite{amm2023bia,sahebjamnia2015integrated,assetcriticality2023,raptaki2025propagation,vonderassen2024quanttm}. No study reports a measured improvement in recovery during real incidents, so the evidence supports plausibility and feasibility more than effectiveness. Replication materials are generally not published: two studies offer data on request \cite{container2021bia,assetcriticality2023}, and a third states that no datasets were generated or analyzed although it reports a case analysis \cite{raptaki2025propagation}.

Methods therefore exist that estimate impact, tolerability, and criticality from judgement, operating data, or probabilistic models, and some derive tolerable periods from time-indexed assessments. The reviewed studies, however, are tied to one organization, depend on case-specific scales or data, and do not publish the interpretation rules, checks, and configurable parameters that would let another organization reproduce a service-level assessment. The use of such assessments to rank services is considered in Section~\ref{sec:lr_prioritization}.

\section{Service Criticality and Recovery Prioritization}
\label{sec:lr_prioritization}

Once impact and tolerability have been assessed, a further step is needed to turn them into decisions: which services are critical, and in what relative order they should be recovered. The two outputs are related but distinct, since a classification places a service in a class whereas a ranking orders services against one another. Section~\ref{sec:lr_bia} dealt with how impact and time parameters are obtained, and this section turns to how they are converted into a classification or a ranking. Section~\ref{sec:lr_mcdm} examines multi-criteria approaches, Section~\ref{sec:lr_rules} rule-based approaches, and Section~\ref{sec:lr_sequencing} the scheduling studies that mark the boundary of the proposed work.

\subsection{Multi-Criteria Approaches}
\label{sec:lr_mcdm}

Multi-criteria methods combine several assessed criteria into one score or ranking. In the continuity studies reviewed they take the form of pairwise-comparison weighting, fuzzy techniques, and probabilistic scoring. Sahebjamnia et al.\ use the analytic hierarchy process to obtain the relative importance of products, which then enter their optimization model as weights \cite{sahebjamnia2015integrated}. Aghabegloo et al.\ go further. They weight the criteria for selecting a key product with the fuzzy best--worst method, select it with fuzzy TOPSIS from the comparisons of four managers, and then compute for each supporting asset an index that sums, over sustainability and resilience factors, the weighted probability that each factor is critical \cite{assetcriticality2023}. A Bayesian network built from fuzzy DEMATEL judgements links the factors, and a contributor analysis ranks which factors most affect the index \cite{assetcriticality2023}.

These properties are real advantages. The methods accommodate several dimensions of impact and the knowledge of experts, they represent uncertainty explicitly, and the contributor analysis of Aghabegloo et al.\ gives a partial account of why an asset ranks where it does \cite{assetcriticality2023}. Their limits are also documented by the studies themselves. The weights and links come from small groups of respondents, and the structure of the network depends on thresholds chosen by the authors: in the gas-plant case a link threshold had to be raised from 0.50 to 0.52 to remove a cycle \cite{assetcriticality2023}. Validation consisted of expert face-validity questions and a sensitivity analysis, which support plausibility and not the accuracy of the ranking in a real incident, and the number of assets analyzed is reported inconsistently \cite{assetcriticality2023}. A continuity self-assessment tool offers a comparable case on a different object. Its nested weights were derived from a literature review and expert input, its scores are shown on a scale from 0 to 100, and its evaluation with a five-person focus group and interviews in ten organizations supports perceived usefulness and not an effect on recovery \cite{selfassessment2025}.

Coarse ordinal scoring has the opposite weakness. In the preprint of von der Assen et al.\ a three-by-three risk matrix rated all six threat scenarios as medium, and a second ordinal scheme rated almost all of them medium to high, which the authors take as evidence that such scales discriminate poorly \cite{vonderassen2024quanttm}. The comparison rests on one expert and has no ground truth, so it is indicative only.

In the studies reviewed, multi-criteria methods are therefore well established for ranking products and assets by judgement, but another organization would have to elicit the weights and the model structure anew, and none of the studies applies them to assign recovery requirements to services from time-indexed impact ratings.

\subsection{Rule-Based Approaches}
\label{sec:lr_rules}

Rule-based approaches classify or rank by explicit thresholds, matrices, or tiers. They are the form taken by most guidance. The DGA guideline selects the highest impact level among its factors and maps each level to a recovery time, and the National Framework presents classes ranging from zero to eight hours up to two weeks as illustrative (Section~\ref{sec:lr_saudi}). NIST IR 8179 allows a ranking, a measure such as time lost or cost, or ranges and thresholds, and one of its example schemes assigns the highest criticality to the items affected by the largest number of scenarios \cite{nist2018criticality}. Documentation of commercial platforms describes rules of the same kind. One platform computes the recovery time objective as the minimum of the downtimes found for the impact categories, applies a configured maximum when no category reaches the intolerable level, and orders rating labels alphanumerically, which matters when the ratings are not monotonic \cite{servicenow2025rto}. Another consolidates the assessments of several departments by average, weighted average, maximum, or minimum, with the same rule for all impact types or a different one for each \cite{globalsuite2025prebia}. These tools are discussed in Section~\ref{sec:lr_tools}.

An explicit rule has properties that a scoring model fitted to expert judgement lacks. The path from input to output can be inspected, the parameters can in principle be changed without altering the logic, and the same inputs should yield the same result. The evidence that these properties hold in practice is thin. The national documents are guidance and report no evaluation, and the vendor documentation describes behavior that was not tested \cite{servicenow2025rto,globalsuite2025prebia}. The self-assessment tool shows how details of a rule can be left open. Its bands are red below 60, yellow from 60 to below 90, and green from 90 to 100, yet one passage prints an open upper endpoint for the green band while the stated definition of an accepted score includes 100, and the calculation depends on weights that must be normalized, which the printed tables do not state \cite{selfassessment2025}. In the guidance and documentation examined, how ties between services are broken and how incomplete assessments are handled are, with the exceptions noted above, not specified.

Explicit rules are not evidence of correct execution. None of the rule-based sources reviewed reports a verification of its rules against independently derived expected results, and where evaluation exists it concerns perceived usefulness \cite{selfassessment2025}. A study of a rule-based method would therefore need to separate three claims: that the rules are executed as specified, that users interpret the results as intended, and that the outcomes support better decisions.

\subsection{Recovery Sequencing}
\label{sec:lr_sequencing}

Ranking services by importance is not the same as scheduling their recovery, and the scheduling literature shows what the latter additionally requires. El-Shekeil et al.\ plan the order in which servers of an enterprise data center are restored so that the weighted availability of user-request types over the whole recovery period is maximized \cite{elshekeil2017precesion}. Their model has three layers of user requests, services, and servers, with prerequisites inside each layer and between layers, restoration phases that need a named expert, and a small pool of workers. The problem is shown to be NP-hard and is solved with a genetic algorithm that, on synthetic instances, reached 99 percent of the optimum while running more than 2,800 times faster; a real data center with 15 request types, 60 services, and 107 servers was also analyzed \cite{elshekeil2017precesion}. Even a small worked example in the paper shows that shared prerequisite services force certain servers to be restored first, whatever the importance of the requests that wait on them \cite{elshekeil2017precesion}. The model of Sahebjamnia et al., described in Section~\ref{sec:lr_bia_approaches}, likewise allocates resources to operating levels across periods under budget and capacity constraints \cite{sahebjamnia2015integrated}.

Two points matter for this thesis. First, both models take importance as given, through weights proportional to the number of users in the first case and weights obtained with the analytic hierarchy process in the second \cite{elshekeil2017precesion,sahebjamnia2015integrated}; a relative priority of services is the kind of input they assume and not the schedule they produce. Second, an executable schedule needs information that a priority ranking does not contain, namely prerequisite order, restoration durations, and worker capacity. The accuracy of the first model was demonstrated on synthetic scenarios and not on the real data center, and neither study compares its plans with rule-based prioritization \cite{elshekeil2017precesion,sahebjamnia2015integrated}. The proposed methodology stays on the side of relative priority and does not generate recovery schedules.

\section{Dependency Modeling}
\label{sec:lr_dependencies}

The proposed methodology links each assessed service to the dependencies needed to deliver it and gives those dependencies a criticality derived from what they support. Section~\ref{sec:lr_service_dependency} therefore examines how the dependencies of services are represented in guidance, tools, and studies. Section~\ref{sec:lr_interdependency} considers wider interdependency models only to mark the boundary of the proposed association, and Section~\ref{sec:lr_criticality} compares the documented ways of valuing supporting resources.

\subsection{Service Dependencies}
\label{sec:lr_service_dependency}

Continuity guidance treats the identification of dependencies as part of the BIA. NIST IR 8286D describes the analysis as an opportunity to record interdependencies and gives the example of a network router that matters to an enterprise because it enables a vital sales website; recording critical systems together with the infrastructure they depend on is named as one of the most common uses of the analysis \cite{quinn2025bia}. NIST IR 8179 asks for dependencies to be mapped at the program, system, and component levels, and in its bottom-up review it looks for components that are used in several subsystems and for components obtained from a single supplier \cite{nist2018criticality}. The DGA guideline likewise lists a missing dependency map among common planning mistakes \cite{dga2025guidelineen}.

Tools implement the same idea in different forms. One commercial platform links assets, dependencies, plans, and events in a configuration-management database \cite{servicenow2025datasheet}, another maps relationships among people, processes, places, systems, and third parties, including upstream and downstream dependencies \cite{fusion2026bcm}, and a third describes dependency mapping within its BIA and planning functions \cite{riskonnect2026bcm}. The GRASP documentation shows an assessment in which linked assets, service providers, and upstream and downstream process chains are checked for single points of failure, and requirements for redundancy or alternative processes are derived \cite{grasp2025bia}. In a research setting, El-Shekeil et al.\ represent dependencies as binary matrices between user requests, services, and servers and within each layer, so that a service is available only when all the servers it needs have been restored \cite{elshekeil2017precesion}.

These representations answer the question of what supports what. They do not by themselves establish recovery precedence, resource capacity, or a feasible schedule. The scheduling study shows that the order of restoration depends in addition on durations and on the number of qualified workers (Section~\ref{sec:lr_sequencing}), and the product pages reviewed disclose no calculation, such as an inheritance rule, that uses their dependency data \cite{fusion2026bcm,riskonnect2026bcm}. A service-to-dependency association of the kind needed here is therefore a statement of support and not an operational plan.

\subsection{Interdependency Models}
\label{sec:lr_interdependency}

Research on interdependency has largely concerned infrastructures. Rinaldi et al.\ propose a taxonomy in which dependencies are physical, cyber, geographic, or logical, distinguish a dependency from a bidirectional interdependency, and separate cascading, escalating, and common-cause failures; they also note that dependencies may differ between normal operation, stress, and repair \cite{rinaldi2001interdependencies}. The article is a conceptual synthesis built from historical cases and workshops, it reports no validated ranking or recovery algorithm, and it describes the state of the field in 2001 \cite{rinaldi2001interdependencies}.

A data-driven alternative is the process-mining system of Raptaki et al., which infers dependencies between activities from event logs and propagates the relative impact of an incident from a chosen starting activity through a weighted graph, flagging activities above a threshold of 0.5 in its case \cite{raptaki2025propagation}. The system is implemented, exports its ranking and graph, and was assessed through scenarios and interviews with five employees \cite{raptaki2025propagation}. It shows that automated and inspectable dependency analysis already exists, but its scores are relative to the starting point of each scenario and are not the criticality of a service.

These works address failure propagation, that is, how a disruption or its impact travels. The proposed association asks a different question, namely what importance a supporting resource derives from the services it supports. It does not model cascading failure, simulate disruptions, or estimate the speed of propagation, and the wider infrastructure literature is therefore reviewed here only to mark that boundary.

\subsection{Criticality of Dependencies}
\label{sec:lr_criticality}

The principle that supporting resources take on the importance of what they support is documented. NIST IR 8286D states that assets inherit the priority of the functions they support, but it gives this as a principle and prescribes no rule for an asset that supports several functions \cite{quinn2025bia}. NIST IR 8179 analyses criticality at several levels and traces it across shared components, using rankings, measures, or ranges and thresholds defined by the organization, but the passages examined do not show the rule applied to a component that serves several systems \cite{nist2018criticality}. Among the tools, the classic verinice product is described as inheriting business-impact values automatically through an asset tree, yet the available description does not say whether the inheritance takes the maximum, a weighted combination, or another form, or whether it applies in the continuity domain \cite{verinice2025bcm}. Documentation of a commercial platform describes the derivation of dependencies in its BIA workflow without disclosing an inheritance rule \cite{servicenow2025bia}.

Two points follow. First, inherited criticality is used here in the sense of the importance a dependency derives from the services it supports. It is not an inherited recovery priority, which, as Section~\ref{sec:lr_sequencing} showed, also depends on prerequisites, durations, and capacity. Second, the sources reviewed support the principle of inheritance but not a particular aggregation rule. The default proposed for TDRP, that a shared dependency takes the highest criticality among the services it supports, is conservative in that it never assigns a dependency less importance than any service it supports. It may also overstate the importance of a resource that has an equivalent substitute for its most critical consumer, and weighted or additive combinations are conceivable alternatives. Which rule is appropriate is therefore a matter for evaluation and for the policy of each organization, and not a requirement established by the literature.

\section{Decision Support Systems for Business Continuity}
\label{sec:lr_dss}

Masar is a decision support system, so it must be placed within research on such systems and in relation to the software available for continuity management. Section~\ref{sec:lr_dss_foundations} outlines the relevant concepts, Section~\ref{sec:lr_dss_domain} examines published systems and prototypes, and Section~\ref{sec:lr_tools} compares documented software.

\subsection{Foundations}
\label{sec:lr_dss_foundations}

Power organizes the history of decision support around five categories: model-driven systems that manipulate quantitative models, data-driven systems that retrieve and analyze organizational data, communications-driven systems that support group decisions, document-driven systems that retrieve text, and knowledge-driven systems that apply specialized domain knowledge to suggest actions, for which rule-based expert systems are the historical example \cite{power2007history}. The categories overlap and describe a dominant mechanism, and the web is a delivery platform across them and not a further category \cite{power2007history}. Arnott et al.\ define the field as the part of information systems concerned with supporting and improving managerial decision-making, and their review of 926 articles from 1990 to 2003 places intelligent systems, which apply techniques from artificial intelligence, at 13.7 percent of the sample \cite{arnott2005descriptive}.

The same review is critical. It found the primary client unclear in 88.8 percent of the articles and the primary user unclear in 57.3 percent, almost half of the articles cited no research on judgement and decision-making, and practical relevance was rated low or absent in more than half \cite{arnott2005descriptive}. The sample is limited to journals and ends in 2003, and its coding of relevance is subjective, so these figures describe an earlier period \cite{arnott2005descriptive}. They nevertheless give a methodological reason for a system such as Masar to state its users and the decisions it supports.

Four elements of a decision support system are worth separating for the purposes of this thesis: the data that the system stores, the rules that calculate from them, including the parameters an organization configures, the explanations that connect a displayed result to its inputs, and the human authority that makes the decision. This separation is the thesis's own and is not drawn from a single source. The sources reviewed describe categories of systems and do not offer an evaluated account of how to arrange these elements in a continuity system. How Masar is classified is a matter for the design chapters, and a rule-based design should not be taken to imply learning, autonomous decisions, or proven effectiveness.

\subsection{Decision Support in Continuity and Disaster Management}
\label{sec:lr_dss_domain}

Published systems that support continuity decisions are few, and they differ in the decision they address. The preprint of von der Assen et al.\ describes a browser-based prototype that supports BIA for threat scenarios: the user enters recovery levels and time frames with financial estimates, and the tool plots losses, recovery trajectories, and the shares of tangible and intangible impact \cite{vonderassen2024quanttm}. The system of Raptaki et al.\ is a desktop application that builds a process graph from event logs, propagates impact, and exports ranked activities and the weighted graph \cite{raptaki2025propagation}. Russo et al.\ present a self-assessment tool in which 61 weighted questions yield scores from 0 to 100 for organizational preparedness and guide decisions about where to invest in improvement \cite{selfassessment2025}. The recovery-planning algorithm of El-Shekeil et al.\ and the optimization model of Sahebjamnia et al.\ are decision models and not interactive systems \cite{elshekeil2017precesion,sahebjamnia2015integrated}.

The supported decisions differ accordingly: the first prioritizes threat scenarios by expected loss, the second ranks activities for risk mitigation, and the third guides investment in preparedness. In the systems reviewed, none derives service-level tolerable periods and recovery times from configurable rules or assigns criticality to supporting dependencies, and none was evaluated by comparing its outputs with independently specified expected results. Evaluation consisted of case use, focus groups, interviews, and expert comparison with five or ten participants (Section~\ref{sec:lr_methods}). The dashboard planned for Masar differs from these systems in its role: it presents recorded results and their context to authorized users during an interruption, and it does not simulate incidents or recommend actions.

Two platforms document temporal rules. One computes the recovery time objective as the minimum of the category downtimes derived from configured tolerability and durations, with a configured maximum when no rating is intolerable \cite{servicenow2025rto}. The other lets the analyst enter impact ratings over a time scale and derive the maximum tolerable period by three criteria, consolidate departmental assessments by average, weighted average, maximum, or minimum, and record a minimum operating level \cite{globalsuite2025prebia}. \section{Research Methods and Evaluation}
\label{sec:lr_methods}

The related studies reflect a design-science orientation, whether or not they name it. Hevner et al.\ distinguish artifacts as constructs, models, methods, and instantiations, and note that an implemented prototype demonstrates feasibility but not utility \cite{hevner2004design}, and Peffers et al.\ propose a nominal process for such research \cite{peffers2007dsrm}. Both were introduced in Chapter~\ref{ch:introduction}, so the interest here lies in how related studies applied the approach. Russo et al.\ used a systematic literature review as the problem-identification step of a design-science project \cite{russo2023maturity}. Arias-Aranda et al.\ built a reference model of continuity processes by matching standards and verified it with practitioners (Section~\ref{sec:lr_standards}) \cite{bcms2026reference}. For the choice of evaluation, Venable et al.\ classify evaluation episodes by purpose, formative or summative, and by setting, artificial or naturalistic, and note that control and repeatability do not establish effectiveness in real use \cite{venable2016feds}.

The studies reviewed in Sections~\ref{sec:lr_bia} to~\ref{sec:lr_dss} evaluate their artifacts in a few recurring ways. Most use a worked or single case, sometimes with a hypothetical disruption \cite{amm2023bia,container2021bia,sahebjamnia2015integrated,assetcriticality2023}. Others gather the views of small groups: a five-person focus group with a usability scale \cite{vonderassen2024quanttm}, five interviews \cite{raptaki2025propagation}, and a five-person focus group with interviews in ten organizations \cite{selfassessment2025}. Comparison with alternatives is made by one expert \cite{vonderassen2024quanttm} or against other propagation approaches \cite{raptaki2025propagation}. The algorithmic study benchmarks its heuristic against an exact solver on synthetic instances, and reports no optimality gap for its real data \cite{elshekeil2017precesion}. One framework adds a sensitivity analysis \cite{assetcriticality2023}.

In the terms of Venable et al., this evidence is largely artificial and formative or small-scale naturalistic. It addresses plausibility, perceived usefulness and usability, and, in one study, closeness to an optimum on synthetic cases. Few of the studies check outputs against specified test cases, the sensitivity of results to valid configurations, the handling of boundary and missing data, the treatment of shared dependencies, or the traceability of outputs to inputs. None reports effectiveness in real disruptions, and replication materials are generally unavailable (Section~\ref{sec:lr_bia_approaches}).

Two lessons for evaluating a rule-based methodology follow. Controlled cases can test deterministic output properties, whereas practitioner tasks examine usefulness and interpretation, so each evaluation episode should define the property evaluated, the reference criterion, the setting, the participants or data, and the limit of inference \cite{venable2016feds}. A synthetic demonstration should not be called naturalistic because it resembles a real organization, and interviews alone do not establish computational correctness. These lessons concern the design of the evaluation, which is described in the methodology chapter.

\section{Synthesis and Research Gap}
\label{sec:lr_gap}

Table~\ref{tab:lr_comparison_matrix} brings together the closest approaches reviewed in this chapter and sets them against the intended features of TDRP and Masar. The comparison is limited to what the reviewed documentation and studies state.

\input{tables/lr_comparison_matrix}

The review contains findings that argue against any claim of general novelty. Commercial platforms document temporal rules that yield recovery objectives, and they offer configurable scales (Section~\ref{sec:lr_tools}). The DGA guideline states an explicit rule for classification and recovery time (Section~\ref{sec:lr_saudi}). Inheritance of importance by supporting resources is documented as a principle and, in one tool, as a described feature (Section~\ref{sec:lr_criticality}). Implemented systems with inspectable outputs exist (Section~\ref{sec:lr_dss_domain}). Some of what looks like a gap is a limit of reporting: vendor documentation may not disclose algorithms, and an undocumented feature is not an absent one.

What the sources reviewed do not provide is a documented, configurable, and evaluated combination of five elements. The first is service-level assessments recorded across successive periods and impact dimensions. The second is explicit rules that derive criticality, the maximum tolerable period, and the recovery time from them, together with stated interpretations for incomplete data, values at a threshold, and the case in which no threshold is reached. The third is rules for relative priority that do not claim to be a recovery schedule. The fourth is a stated rule for the criticality of dependencies shared by several services. The fifth is outputs that can be traced to the assessments, configuration, and rules that produced them, together with an evaluation of the correctness, consistency, and traceability of such rules. This is a gap in the sources reviewed, which were selected purposively. It is not a claim about all existing work or all undisclosed product behavior.

The gap relates to the five objectives of the project as follows. For the first objective, time-indexed matrices and temporal rules exist, but the interpretation rules are rarely stated (Sections~\ref{sec:lr_time_impact} and~\ref{sec:lr_parameters}). For the second, ranking methods are well developed, but they work on assets, products, or threat scenarios and do not derive service priorities from tolerable periods and criticality (Section~\ref{sec:lr_prioritization}). For the third, the principle of inheritance is documented while the aggregation rule for shared dependencies is not (Section~\ref{sec:lr_criticality}). For the fourth, related prototypes support other decisions and their role differs from the planned dashboard (Section~\ref{sec:lr_dss_domain}). For the fifth, evaluation in the studies reviewed rests on cases, small groups, and synthetic benchmarks, and none reports a verification of rule logic against specified expected results (Section~\ref{sec:lr_methods}).

The project's contributions can accordingly be described in three groups. The concepts it adopts are the definitions and classifications of the standards and national guidance, the time-indexed impact assessment, and the principle that supporting resources inherit importance. The techniques it adapts are the reading of tolerable periods and recovery times from time-indexed ratings, as documented for commercial tools and in the metering study, and the use of rule-based classification. The rules it proposes are the stated interpretations for incomplete and boundary cases and the highest-criticality default for shared dependencies, both of which have to be evaluated. Prototype-level evidence of the kind this project can produce would not establish that the approach improves the outcome of real incidents.

\section{Chapter Summary}
\label{sec:lr_summary}

This chapter reviewed the literature from continuity management in general to the problem addressed by the thesis. The standards and national guidance define the parameters and expect impact assessment, but they leave open how successive assessments are converted into tolerable periods and recovery times. The analytical studies estimate impact, tolerability, and criticality by judgement, operating data, and probabilistic models, mostly in single cases with limited evaluation and with interpretation rules that are seldom stated. Methods for ranking rely on weighted or rule-based scoring, and the scheduling studies show that a recovery sequence needs information beyond importance. Dependencies are widely recorded, and inheritance of importance is documented as a principle without a specified rule for shared resources. Published decision support systems address different decisions, and commercial and open-source tools document parts of the pipeline without disclosing rules in a form that can be evaluated.

The gap that remains in the reviewed sources concerns a configurable and documented rule set that derives service criticality, tolerable periods, recovery times, and priorities, assigns criticality to shared dependencies, and keeps outputs traceable, together with an evaluation of its correctness, consistency, and traceability. The next chapter describes the methodology developed to address that gap.
