\chapter{Literature Review}
\label{ch:literaturereview}

Business continuity requires organizations to relate the consequences of disruption to the time available for restoring essential services. This chapter examines how that relationship is represented in business impact analysis (BIA), translated into recovery requirements, and communicated through decision support systems (DSS). The discussion develops the conceptual and empirical basis for Time-Dependent Recovery Prioritization (TDRP), the configurable methodology proposed in this project, and for Masar, its web-based implementation. Particular attention is given to the reasoning that connects an assessed business service to its recovery priority and to the criticality of its supporting dependencies.

The review proceeds from the management context to the specific analytical problem. Section~\ref{sec:lr_bcm} examines continuity concepts and guidance. Section~\ref{sec:lr_bia} reviews temporal impact assessment and recovery parameters. Section~\ref{sec:lr_prioritization} compares classification, multi-criteria analysis, and recovery scheduling. Section~\ref{sec:lr_dependencies} considers service dependencies and inherited resource criticality. Section~\ref{sec:lr_dss} examines research applications and comparable software, and Section~\ref{sec:lr_methods} discusses the evaluation evidence. Section~\ref{sec:lr_gap} synthesizes the findings and defines the project's contribution, before Section~\ref{sec:lr_summary} concludes the chapter.

The chapter uses a focused narrative review organized around the research objectives introduced in the preceding chapter. Sources include original research, public institutional guidance, standards catalogue records, and providers' technical documentation. Selection emphasizes relevance to temporal assessment, recovery requirements, service prioritization, dependency analysis, and artifact evaluation. Foundational studies establish concepts, while recent contributions illustrate their application. This purposive coverage supports a comparative synthesis rather than an exhaustive account of the field. Findings reported by researchers, requirements described in guidance, and functions disclosed by software providers are assessed according to their different evidential roles.

\section{Business Continuity Management}
\label{sec:lr_bcm}

\subsection{Continuity and Organizational Resilience}
\label{sec:lr_context}

Business continuity management (BCM) organizes an institution's preparation for maintaining or resuming prioritized activities during disruption. Its subject is the delivery of organizational outcomes, including services to beneficiaries, contractual commitments, and essential operations. A business continuity management system (BCMS) embeds this preparation in an ongoing management process. The public scope of ISO 22301 describes requirements for such a system, placing continuity within organizational governance and improvement rather than equating it with a single recovery plan \cite{iso22301catalogue}. A software application supporting BIA therefore contributes to a wider management capability whose responsibilities remain distributed across the organization.

Information-system recovery addresses an enabling part of that capability. NIST SP 800-34 distinguishes system contingency planning from business continuity and other related plans \cite{nist2010contingency}. Restoring a database may enable a service without completing its recovery: staff may still need to reconcile records, reconnect external providers, or clear a processing backlog. Conversely, a service may temporarily continue through a manual procedure while its normal technology remains unavailable. The distinction establishes why recovery requirements should be derived with reference to business outcomes and then related to technical resources.

Resilience broadens the analysis to an organization's ability to withstand disruption and regain acceptable performance. Sahebjamnia et al. represent continuity and recovery through operating levels over time, connecting the depth of disruption with the resources required for restoration \cite{sahebjamnia2015integrated}. Aghabegloo et al. incorporate absorptive, adaptive, and restorative capacities into physical-asset criticality assessment \cite{assetcriticality2023}. These perspectives locate recovery prioritization within a larger resilience problem. They also indicate that resilience cannot be inferred from a priority score alone: maintaining capacity, adapting operations, and executing recovery require additional organizational capabilities.

BCM research consequently includes different objects of evaluation. Russo et al. review organizational maturity and continuity frameworks, whereas Arias-Aranda et al. identify processes for a BCMS reference model \cite{russo2023maturity,bcms2026reference}. Their contributions concern the organization of continuity work and its assessment. They provide context for a service-prioritization application, but answer a different question from whether a particular rule correctly derives a tolerability deadline. Distinguishing these levels prevents management maturity, computational correctness, and operational performance from being treated as interchangeable outcomes.

\subsection{Standards and Contingency Planning Guidance}
\label{sec:lr_standards}

ISO 22301:2019, ISO 22313:2020, and ISO/TS 22317:2021 address complementary concerns: BCMS requirements, guidance on their application, and guidance on BIA \cite{iso22301catalogue,iso22313catalogue,iso22317catalogue}. The public description of ISO/TS 22317 allows BIA processes to reflect organizational needs, resources, and constraints. Such adaptation is relevant to organizations whose service sensitivity, obligations, and assessment capabilities differ. The catalogue records establish this scope; they do not supply the full standards or support a clause-level compliance assessment.

\input{tables/lr_standards_comparison}

NIST SP 800-34 provides a detailed contingency-planning perspective. Its BIA connects business-process criticality with supporting resources and recovery priorities \cite{nist2010contingency}. This creates a practical relationship between organizational consequences and technical planning. A resource's recovery target should follow from the processes it enables, with sufficient time for the activities that remain after technical restoration. The sequence also reduces the risk of assigning demanding targets to technology simply because it is visible or expensive, while overlooking less prominent resources essential to service delivery.

Guidance and analytical methods have different roles in this relationship. Guidance establishes the purpose of BIA, the information to consider, and the need to identify recovery requirements. An analytical method specifies how particular inputs are interpreted and combined. A documented assessment process may therefore accommodate several computational procedures. Their appropriateness depends on the organization's decision policy and the meaning of the collected evidence. Standards alignment is a basis for designing the process, while reproducibility and correctness require a sufficiently explicit account of the implemented rules.

A further implication concerns governance. Consistent calculations require stable definitions of assessment objects, scales, and time units. Organizational flexibility requires a means of changing those definitions when approved requirements change. A configurable DSS must support both. The structure of the procedure can remain common across organizations while the impact descriptions, thresholds, and recovery mappings reflect locally approved judgments. This distinction between procedural consistency and parameter uniformity is central to the proposed TDRP methodology.

Service assessment informs recovery requirements and relative priorities; recorded support relationships explain dependency criticality. \subsection{Saudi Continuity Guidance and Service Orientation}
\label{sec:lr_saudi}

The Saudi National Framework for Risk, Emergency and Business Continuity Management relates continuity to organizational strategy, essential services, enabling activities, and recovery requirements \cite{nrcnationalframework}. Its BCM material considers multiple consequences of interruption and permits assessment through interviews, workshops, and questionnaires. This approach recognizes that continuity information is partly organizational knowledge: the significance of losing a service may depend on obligations, beneficiaries, and activities that are not evident from a technical inventory.

The Digital Government Authority (DGA) similarly connects BIA with time-sensitive impacts, approved assessment criteria, and required recovery arrangements \cite{dga2025bcm,dga2025guidelineen}. The English guideline includes platform-impact classes and associated recovery targets, using the highest classification when impact factors differ. This provides an example of a non-compensatory decision policy: a serious consequence in one dimension retains its significance despite lower assessments elsewhere. It also demonstrates that explicit impact classification and recovery mapping already have institutional precedents.

The Saudi material supports a service-oriented interpretation of the research problem. A service is assessed because its interruption affects an organizational outcome. Enabling resources become relevant through their contribution to that service. Nevertheless, government examples must be interpreted in context. Assessment intervals and recovery classes illustrated in guidance are not sufficient grounds for imposing identical settings on every organization. The selection of thresholds remains an accountable judgment concerning what consequences are acceptable and for how long.

Taken together, the international and national sources establish a coherent management basis for Masar. The system should preserve the connection between business consequences, approved recovery requirements, and supporting resources. Implementing selected BIA functions does not establish a complete BCMS or certify compliance. The research contribution must instead be assessed through the quality of the specified prioritization procedure and the evidence supporting its implementation.

\section{Business Impact Analysis and Recovery Requirements}
\label{sec:lr_bia}

\subsection{Assessment Objects and Sources of Evidence}
\label{sec:lr_bia_process}

BIA evaluates the consequences of interruption in relation to organizational objectives. Its unit of analysis varies across the literature. NIST connects business processes and supporting systems; Horalek examines automated meter management (AMM) data; Budiyanto et al. study electricity disruption at a container terminal; and Aghabegloo et al. assess built physical assets through their contribution to key products \cite{nist2010contingency,amm2023bia,container2021bia,assetcriticality2023}. These studies share an interest in business consequences, but their scores and recovery quantities refer to different objects. Comparison requires identifying the assessed object before interpreting a reported result.

For service recovery prioritization, a useful assessment boundary encompasses the outcome delivered to beneficiaries and the activities necessary to sustain it. Excessively broad boundaries may conceal activities with different deadlines. Excessively narrow boundaries may fragment one service into duplicate assessments and obscure shared obligations. A service definition should therefore identify its output, owner, beneficiaries, and normal or minimum delivery expectations. This is a modeling implication of adopting a service-level BIA, rather than an assertion that the reviewed studies use identical service definitions.

Evidence is commonly assembled from both operational information and expert judgment. Horalek combines technical examination with guided interviews involving knowledgeable participants \cite{amm2023bia}. Budiyanto et al. combine management assessments and a focus group with operating records \cite{container2021bia}. Records can anchor estimates of throughput loss or interruption duration. Interviews help identify consequences involving reputation, contractual exposure, and service users. Neither source is sufficient in isolation: records may omit unobserved scenarios, while judgments may reflect inconsistent interpretations or departmental interests.

A defensible assessment retains the rationale behind each rating. NIST IR 8286D connects BIA with mission objectives, asset significance, and recorded impact information used in risk communication \cite{quinn2025bia}. The implication for a DSS is that a numerical cell should remain associated with its meaning and provenance. An approved legal-impact assessment, for example, is more interpretable when its underlying obligation is recorded. This also supports later review when a service, policy, or assessment assumption changes.

\subsection{Impact Dimensions and Temporal Representation}
\label{sec:lr_time_impact}

Disruption can affect finances, operations, legal obligations, reputation, and beneficiaries. These dimensions do not necessarily share a natural measurement unit. Financial effects may be estimated in currency, whereas harm to confidence or service quality may be expressed through ordered categories. The container-terminal study demonstrates contextual assessment using operational and economic consequences alongside management judgments \cite{container2021bia}. Such evidence supports a multidimensional BIA while cautioning against assuming that the same rating has identical meaning across different organizations.

Temporal representation adds the duration of interruption to this assessment. Horalek evaluates availability-related consequences at seven horizons, from thirty minutes to more than one week \cite{amm2023bia}. This distinguishes services or information assets that eventually reach similar severity but do so at different rates. A static label of high impact can conceal a substantial difference between a service whose consequences become unacceptable within hours and another whose effects accumulate over several days. Recovery urgency depends on this temporal distinction.

A time-indexed matrix is an accessible way to represent the assessment. Each cell associates an impact dimension and disruption duration with a rating. The durations must be ordered explicitly, and their spacing matters. A matrix using hourly observations provides different temporal resolution from one using daily observations. The observations remain judgments at specified durations; they do not establish a continuous impact function. Interpolating an exact threshold-crossing time between cells would require assumptions beyond the matrix itself.

The shape of an impact trajectory also depends on what is being measured. Accumulated financial loss and the current rate of operational harm can behave differently. Deadlines may create abrupt increases, while temporary alternatives may change the consequences of an ongoing outage. A rule that requires non-decreasing ratings is consequently an assessment policy that needs justification. Data validation should distinguish an impossible value from a trajectory that is unusual but has a documented business explanation.

Temporal completeness is equally consequential. A blank cell may represent an unfinished assessment, unavailable evidence, or an inapplicable category. Treating it as negligible impact would introduce an unsupported assumption. Similarly, no unacceptable rating within the observed horizon establishes only that the boundary was not identified there. The resulting uncertainty should remain visible. These interpretive requirements follow from bounded observations and are especially important when a matrix is converted into a precise-looking recovery requirement.

\subsection{Tolerability and Recovery Objectives}
\label{sec:lr_parameters}

Maximum tolerable period of disruption (MTPD) concerns the boundary beyond which interruption consequences become unacceptable. Recovery time objective (RTO) specifies a target for resumption. The two quantities serve related planning purposes but have different meanings. NIST uses maximum tolerable downtime (MTD) for a business process and RTO for the recovery of supporting system resources, allowing for work recovery after restoration \cite{nist2010contingency}. The Saudi guidance also identifies MTPD and RTO among BIA outputs \cite{nrcnationalframework,dga2025guidelineen}. Their interpretation must retain the relevant service, process, or resource boundary.

A derived RTO states a target. Establishing the capability to meet it requires examination of recovery arrangements, available capacity, and exercises. Tightening the value in a system cannot shorten restoration by itself. Conversely, allowing a target to follow only existing technical capability may leave an unacceptable gap against business needs. A DSS should preserve this distinction so that requirements can inform planning and capability gaps remain identifiable.

Recovery point objective (RPO) addresses acceptable data loss through the point to which data should be recoverable. It is a backward-looking data requirement, rather than another duration simply added to the outage budget \cite{nist2010contingency}. Minimum business continuity objective (MBCO) concerns an acceptable level of continued delivery \cite{dga2025guidelineen}. These quantities complement time-based requirements: meeting a nominal deadline is insufficient if the resumed service lacks the data or capacity necessary for its required function. TDRP focuses on criticality, MTPD, RTO, and relative priority, while the wider terminology clarifies its boundary.

Discrete assessment introduces a further issue when identifying MTPD. The first observed unacceptable rating identifies an assessment point at which tolerability has been exceeded or reached according to the chosen convention. It does not establish precisely when the change occurred between observations. The method must state whether it reports that point, a preceding interval, or another approved bound. A recovery mapping then requires its own justification. The threshold interpretation and the RTO mapping should be separately inspectable, even when both are executed by one application.

\subsection{Analytical Approaches in Related Studies}
\label{sec:lr_bia_models}

The reviewed studies differ in what they seek to produce from impact evidence. Horalek uses contextual impact criteria and temporal matrices to establish requirements for AMM data and systems \cite{amm2023bia}. Budiyanto et al. characterize the consequences of a particular infrastructure disruption \cite{container2021bia}. Both demonstrate the importance of grounding BIA in an operating environment, although neither establishes a universally transferable parameter configuration. Table~\ref{tab:lr_bia_studies} compares these contributions with more elaborate analytical approaches.

\input{tables/lr_bia_studies}

QuantTM models business consequences through financial loss, distinguishing persistent impacts from one-time effects and representing recovery in stages \cite{vonderassen2024quanttm}. This allows recovery trajectories to influence loss estimates. Its expected-loss output also depends on threat assumptions, including probabilities. Temporal service assessment and threat quantification therefore overlap in their treatment of time but retain different decision objects. A tolerability requirement identifies when consequences become unacceptable; expected loss combines consequences with uncertainty about the threat.

Aghabegloo et al. combine fuzzy multi-criteria methods and a Bayesian network to evaluate physical-asset criticality, while Raptaki et al. use process mining to derive relationships for impact propagation \cite{assetcriticality2023,raptaki2025propagation}. These methods extend analysis beyond individual matrix cells. Their added explanatory capability comes with requirements for elicitation, model structure, or event data. Selecting an approach consequently involves matching its assumptions and inputs to the decision, rather than treating methodological complexity as evidence of superior suitability.

Across these approaches, temporal BIA supplies a connection between organizational judgments and recovery planning, but the connection takes different forms. Some models elicit tolerability as an input; others use ratings to identify a boundary; optimization models use requirements to construct plans. The research question for TDRP lies at the transformation from approved service assessments to explicit recovery requirements and priorities. Maintaining that location in the analytical chain enables a meaningful comparison with methods that operate before or after it.

\section{Service Criticality and Recovery Prioritization}
\label{sec:lr_prioritization}

\subsection{Classification and Multi-Criteria Analysis}
\label{sec:lr_mcdm}

Criticality expresses the significance of interruption relative to an organization's criteria. A criticality class groups services judged to have comparable significance, while a priority relation distinguishes their relative urgency for recovery. These outputs need not be identical. Several services may belong to the same class yet have different recovery requirements, and services with equal decision-relevant attributes may remain tied. The intended interpretation should be established before selecting an aggregation or ranking technique.

Multi-criteria decision-making methods make trade-offs among consequences explicit. Aghabegloo et al. first identify key products through fuzzy best--worst weighting and fuzzy TOPSIS, then evaluate supporting assets through fuzzy DEMATEL and a Bayesian network \cite{assetcriticality2023}. The business-derived structure is significant: the assets are interpreted through their contribution to important outcomes. The approach also accommodates relationships among factors affecting criticality, rather than assuming independent criteria whose ratings can be combined without further examination.

In the gas-plant application, expert judgments, contextual information, and scenarios support an asset-criticality index and analysis of influential contributors \cite{assetcriticality2023}. Expert face validation and sensitivity analysis provide evidence about the model's plausibility and behavior. The resulting index supports attention to physical assets and continuity investment. It does not have the same meaning as a service deadline identified from successive impact ratings. This difference limits direct numerical comparison while preserving the study's relevance to the derivation of resource importance.

Aggregation policy determines which trade-offs a method permits. A weighted average can allow several low ratings to offset one severe consequence. Such compensation may be defensible for an overall utility judgment, but conflict with a policy under which a legal or safety consequence is decisive. Fuzzy representation addresses imprecision in judgments; it does not establish that compensation is appropriate. Likewise, probabilistic output relies on a defensible interpretation of the modeled variables and their relationships.

For ordinal scales, arithmetic operations introduce additional assumptions. The order of categories may be clear even when the distance between adjacent categories is not. Averaging codes as though those distances were equal therefore requires justification. A categorical threshold rule avoids that particular assumption but introduces others, including threshold selection and treatment of equality. The appropriate comparison concerns the policy represented by each method and the evidence available to sustain its assumptions.

\subsection{Temporal Rules and Configurable Decisions}
\label{sec:lr_rules}

Rule-based methods express decisions through explicit conditions, precedence relationships, and mappings. In temporal BIA, a rule can identify an unacceptable assessment point, assign criticality, or associate a class with a recovery target. The DGA guideline's highest-impact classification provides one institutional example \cite{dga2025guidelineen}. Its non-compensatory character preserves a decisive severe consequence. Applied in software, this reasoning can produce a traceable explanation identifying the impact dimension responsible for the classification.

Documented commercial calculations provide closer computational precedents. GlobalSuite supports configurable temporal assessments and automatic MTPD criteria, including a selected impact level, an average, or a specific impact type \cite{globalsuite2025prebia}. ServiceNow documents category-level tolerability calculations, aggregation through the lowest tolerable downtime, and a configured fallback when all ratings are tolerable \cite{servicenow2025rto}. These precedents show that temporal rule execution and configuration are established capabilities against which TDRP must be positioned.

Small semantic differences can produce substantively different outputs. Selecting the first chronological threshold breach differs from selecting the first impact category according to its stored code. Selecting the maximum across dimensions differs from averaging them. Reporting an unresolved deadline differs from assigning a configured fallback duration. Each procedure can be reproducible. The distinction lies in what its result means and whether that meaning agrees with the organization's approved policy.

A complete rule specification should therefore identify valid inputs and exceptional states as well as normal calculations. Relevant issues include threshold equality, unordered or duplicate durations, missing categories, and assessment horizons that do not reach unacceptable impact. The policy also needs a defined relation between criticality and time urgency. Where rules leave services tied, a stable display order may be useful, but a record identifier should not be presented as a substantive reason that one service deserves earlier recovery.

Configurability makes historical interpretation particularly important. If a threshold changes, recalculating an unchanged assessment may alter its priority. Users need to know which configuration produced the displayed result and whether that result was subsequently approved. NIST's emphasis on recorded BIA values and rationale supports retaining such decision context \cite{quinn2025bia}. For Masar, connecting assessment versions, approved parameters, and derived outputs is a direct implication of reproducible decision support.

\subsection{Ranking and Resource-Constrained Scheduling}
\label{sec:lr_sequencing}

A priority ranking orders services by selected business criteria. A recovery schedule allocates actions and resources over time under operational constraints. The distinction becomes clear when a highly urgent service requires a shared infrastructure component: restoring the prerequisite first may be necessary to recover the service at all. NIST's contingency-planning guidance recognizes the importance of supporting resources and restoration relationships \cite{nist2010contingency}. Business urgency can inform execution, but cannot determine a feasible sequence without information about those constraints.

Sahebjamnia et al. formulate an integrated continuity and disaster recovery model with multiple objectives, operating levels, resource limits, and budgets \cite{sahebjamnia2015integrated}. BIA supplies tolerability and minimum continuity requirements. The planning model then examines how resources can maintain and restore operations across discrete periods. Its manufacturing case demonstrates trade-offs through modeled disruption scenarios. This contribution occupies a later decision stage than identifying requirements from temporal assessments.

PRECESION addresses progressive restoration in enterprise data centers through user-request, service, and server relationships \cite{elshekeil2017precesion}. Limited worker availability, skills, restoration durations, and prerequisites constrain the recovery sequence. The model uses given importance weights and searches for schedules that improve weighted availability over the recovery horizon. It illustrates why dependency structure and capacity can dominate the executable order even when the relative importance of business services has already been established.

The comparison suggests complementary uses rather than a single hierarchy of methods. A temporal BIA method supplies defensible requirements and relative business priorities. A scheduling method can use such information alongside technical and resource constraints. Demonstrating correctness at the first stage does not establish optimality at the second. TDRP's stated outputs consequently support planning and practitioner interpretation, while operational recovery sequencing remains outside the proposed method's analytical scope.

\section{Dependencies and Supporting Resource Criticality}
\label{sec:lr_dependencies}

\subsection{Modeling Service Support Relationships}
\label{sec:lr_service_dependency}

Services depend on activities, personnel, technology, facilities, information, and external providers. Recording these relationships connects the service-level assessment to the resources that make delivery possible. NIST's BIA and the Saudi guidance both recognize this connection \cite{nist2010contingency,nrcnationalframework,dga2025guidelineen}. A service-first representation is especially useful for shared dependencies, whose significance may be distributed across organizational units and therefore difficult to infer from a single technical or departmental perspective.

A dependency relation needs a defined direction and interpretation. A service requiring a database is different from a service merely exchanging optional information with it. Required support may also admit substitution, reduced capacity, or a manual alternative. An association can document that the relationship warrants consideration without specifying the conditions under which its loss disables the service. The level of inference supported by the model depends on this distinction.

PRECESION uses more detailed relationships because its recovery calculations require availability conditions across layers \cite{elshekeil2017precesion}. Those conditions determine which restored servers make particular services and requests available. A simpler inventory linking services to resources can adequately explain inherited criticality, but additional information is necessary for comparable restoration analysis. The representation should be chosen according to the intended output rather than expanded solely to create a more elaborate diagram.

Dependency inventories also have an evidence boundary. An unrecorded relationship may indicate incomplete elicitation rather than independence. A map should therefore retain information about its scope and review status. For decision support, this means that apparent coverage and confirmed coverage should remain distinguishable. The inference follows from using elicited organizational relationships: the absence of a recorded edge cannot be treated automatically as evidence that no supporting requirement exists.

\subsection{Interdependency and Impact Propagation}
\label{sec:lr_interdependency}

Rinaldi et al. distinguish physical, cyber, geographic, and logical infrastructure interdependencies \cite{rinaldi2001interdependencies}. Their framework shows why organizational exposure extends beyond direct technical connections. Resources may share a location or an external utility, or be coupled through information exchange and organizational arrangements. This broader perspective is useful when identifying dependencies because common exposure can affect services that appear separate in an application inventory.

These relationship types do not, by themselves, quantify cascading consequences. A propagation model requires assumptions about operating states, relationship strength, timing, and alternatives. Geographic co-location may indicate that two services face the same event without implying that one service's failure causes the other's. Interpreting a dependency diagram as a causal model would therefore require evidence that a simple inventory does not contain.

Raptaki et al. develop a more analytical approach using process mining \cite{raptaki2025propagation}. Event logs support discovery of a weighted business-process graph, through which incident-related impact is propagated to produce activity rankings. Their application is examined with procurement records from a transportation organization and organizational feedback. The study demonstrates how observed process execution can support analysis beyond manually documented relationships.

The approach's dependence on event data also defines its transfer conditions. Unlogged activities and relationships outside the selected process may be absent from the graph. Its propagated values express relative impact under the model's incident and weighting assumptions. Translating them into MTPD, service criticality, or failure probabilities would require an additional justified mapping. For Masar, the comparison clarifies the narrower evidence needed for recorded support relationships and the additional work that would accompany future propagation analysis.

\subsection{Shared Dependencies and Inherited Criticality}
\label{sec:lr_criticality}

NIST IR 8179 structures criticality analysis across programs, systems, and components, while NIST IR 8286D relates asset significance to enterprise objectives \cite{nist2018criticality,quinn2025bia}. Together they provide prior art for deriving the importance of supporting resources from the outcomes they enable. The service-first principle adopted in this project is thus consistent with an established direction of analysis. Its specific aggregation rule remains a design choice requiring its own justification and evaluation.

The introduction proposes assigning a shared dependency the highest criticality among supported services. This retains the strongest assessed business requirement and prevents an urgent service from being diluted by less critical services. The operation must use the semantic ordering of classes: a numerical maximum is incorrect when smaller codes represent greater criticality. The rule also assumes that the supported services were assessed on a comparable basis under the relevant configuration.

The inherited class answers a limited question about the strongest supported requirement. It does not capture the total number of affected beneficiaries or distinguish resources supporting one critical service from resources supporting many. A count-based measure would answer another question and could overlook a resource essential to a single exceptionally sensitive service. An organization may need both perspectives for planning, but they should remain distinguishable rather than being combined without an explicit interpretation.

Incomplete relationships and assessments require qualified outputs. A dependency linked to an unassessed service has unresolved significance; a dependency with no recorded services has no established service-derived classification. Assigning the lowest class in either case would conceal uncertainty. These cases are methodological implications of inheritance from a partial service model and should be considered separately from the normal case in which every relevant service has an approved assessment.

Recovery timing remains a separate concern. A resource may need restoration before the service RTO to allow configuration, testing, or reconciliation. Inheriting criticality does not determine that preparation interval, and taking a service deadline as the resource deadline may be insufficient. NIST's distinction between process tolerability and system recovery, together with dependency-aware scheduling research, supports retaining these separate meanings \cite{nist2010contingency,elshekeil2017precesion}. Masar's dependency classification explains business significance without claiming a complete engineering plan.

\section{Decision Support Systems and Comparable Applications}
\label{sec:lr_dss}

\subsection{Decision Support and Explanations}
\label{sec:lr_dss_foundations}

DSS research concerns the use of information and analytical capabilities to assist decision makers. Power traces several streams of DSS development, while Arnott et al. examine the methods and application types represented in an earlier period of DSS research \cite{power2007history,arnott2005descriptive}. This literature supports viewing Masar as a combination of structured organizational data, declared analysis, and interactive presentation. The analytical output is useful only in relation to an identifiable decision and a user able to interpret it.

Arnott et al. identify shortcomings in the specification of users and professional relevance within their 1990--2003 sample \cite{arnott2005descriptive}. The historical scope limits claims about current practice, but the concern remains pertinent to artifact design. In this project, continuity practitioners maintain assessments and inspect results, while authorized personnel retain responsibility for recovery decisions. Those roles determine which explanations are necessary and what a practical evaluation should ask participants to do.

A useful explanation connects an output to the entered assessment and the rule that produced it. For a service priority, this may include the decisive impact category, relevant duration, selected recovery mapping, and any unresolved data. For a shared dependency, it includes the services responsible for its inherited class. Such explanations allow practitioners to challenge an input or policy rather than merely accept the displayed number. NIST's emphasis on the rationale accompanying BIA information is consistent with this requirement \cite{quinn2025bia}.

The data and presentation functions must preserve the distinction between evidence and derivation. An entered rating, an approved threshold, and a calculated deadline have different origins even when shown together. Maintaining those origins supports meaningful comparison after updates. This is especially consequential when a dashboard presents historical assessments alongside recalculated results: the interface should expose the context necessary to understand their relationship.

\subsection{Research Applications and Prototype Evidence}
\label{sec:lr_dss_domain}

QuantTM implements threat quantification in a browser-based prototype, connecting technical threat descriptions with business consequences and financial estimates \cite{vonderassen2024quanttm}. Its recovery stages make temporal loss visible, offering a useful example of explanation through both calculations and plots. The reviewed version is an arXiv preprint. Its case-based and small-group evaluation provides exploratory evidence, with limited grounds for generalizing to experienced continuity teams or service-recovery prioritization.

Raptaki et al. integrate process discovery, graph analysis, and export functions in an application for impact propagation \cite{raptaki2025propagation}. The organizational case shows how analytical methods can be connected to operational data. Its value depends on the completeness and interpretation of the event logs, making data coverage part of the explanation of the resulting rankings. This differs from Masar's dependence on approved assessment matrices, although both require users to understand the basis of a derived result.

Russo et al. develop a continuity self-assessment tool using structured questions, scoring, guidance, and visual results \cite{selfassessment2025}. Practitioner engagement supports refinement of the artifact and examination of usefulness. Its output concerns organizational preparedness, which differs from the urgency of restoring individual services. The study is relevant as an evaluation precedent rather than a source of interchangeable scores. It illustrates the need to connect an application's outputs to the particular organizational task being assessed.

These prototypes demonstrate distinct forms of support within continuity and related risk analysis. They also show why a working interface alone is insufficient evidence of decision quality. A prototype may execute its model correctly while users misinterpret a result or supply unsuitable inputs. Evaluation should therefore connect the declared method, the implementation, and users' actual interpretation, with claims limited to the tasks and settings examined.

Their presence narrows the defensible contribution of TDRP: neither web delivery nor configurable temporal calculation is novel in itself.

GRASP GRC documents time-horizon assessment, recovery objectives, supporting assets and providers, and assessment rationale \cite{grasp2025bia}. The verinice BCM domain connects process assessment, BIA, and dependency evaluation within a broader management platform \cite{verinice2025bcm}. These systems demonstrate that the connection among assessments, resources, and organizational management is already represented in practice. The reviewed pages provide less detail about complete derivation semantics and exceptional-input behavior than would be required to reproduce an algorithm.

Fusion and Riskonnect describe integrated continuity-management functions, including BIA and dependency-related planning \cite{fusion2026bcm,riskonnect2026bcm}. Their broad product descriptions support comparison of functional scope but offer limited evidence for detailed algorithmic comparison. Likewise, verinice's open-source history should be distinguished from licensing and deployment conditions for particular product generations. Procurement, deployment, and source-code availability would require product-specific investigation beyond this methodological review.

The platforms collectively establish substantial prior capability. A useful comparison with Masar must consequently examine the exact decision procedure, its transparency, and the evidence supporting its outputs. The proposed system can make its own rules and evaluation inspectable without asserting superiority over products not experimentally tested. This formulation preserves the academic purpose of a bounded artifact while recognizing that continuity practitioners already have access to mature alternatives.

\section{Research Methods and Evaluation Evidence}
\label{sec:lr_methods}

\subsection{Design Science and the Research Artifact}
\label{sec:lr_design_science}

Design-science research provides a framework for developing and assessing artifacts that address organizational problems. Hevner et al. connect relevance and rigor with artifact construction and evaluation \cite{hevner2004design}. Peffers et al. describe activities encompassing problem identification, objectives, design and development, demonstration, evaluation, and communication \cite{peffers2007dsrm}. Applied to this project, the framework requires an explicit connection between the service-prioritization problem, the proposed method, and the evidence used to judge its contribution.

TDRP and Masar represent distinguishable aspects of the artifact. The methodology states the decision rules; the application stores information, executes those rules, and presents results. A methodological error can persist in a perfectly faithful implementation. A programming defect can violate an otherwise coherent specification. A correct result can also be misunderstood by a user. Evaluation should therefore retain these distinctions when interpreting success or failure.

The related literature offers several methodological precedents. Sahebjamnia et al. examine a mathematical model through a manufacturing case and scenarios \cite{sahebjamnia2015integrated}. Aghabegloo et al. use expert input and sensitivity analysis in asset assessment \cite{assetcriticality2023}. Raptaki et al. use organizational process data and feedback, while Russo et al. develop a practitioner-oriented self-assessment artifact \cite{raptaki2025propagation,selfassessment2025}. Each design follows its analytical objective and evidence requirements. Their diversity supports choosing evaluation procedures according to the properties claimed for TDRP.

\subsection{Correctness and Configuration Behavior}
\label{sec:lr_evaluation}

Venable et al.'s Framework for Evaluation in Design Science (FEDS) distinguishes formative from summative evaluation and artificial from naturalistic settings \cite{venable2016feds}. Controlled settings can isolate computational properties, whereas organizational use reveals contextual interpretation and practical constraints. Formative work informs revision; summative work judges performance against declared objectives. The dimensions are complementary rather than interchangeable indicators of research quality.

For deterministic rules, correctness requires expected outcomes derived independently from the specification. Relevant cases include a threshold met exactly, unacceptable impact at the earliest duration, and no observed unacceptable impact. Incomplete assessments, incompatible configurations, tied priorities, and shared dependencies test additional semantic choices. Copying the implementation's output into a reference dataset would provide weak evidence, because the same misunderstanding could be reproduced in both. The expected result needs an independently inspectable rationale.

Consistency concerns properties across executions and controlled changes. Identical inputs and configurations should reproduce identical outputs. Reordering stored assessments should preserve substantive priority unless ordering is explicitly meaningful. If increasing severity is intended never to reduce criticality, valid configurations should preserve that property. These checks test the consequences of the declared method. They cannot establish that an organization's underlying thresholds accurately represent harm, which remains an external judgment.

Sensitivity analysis examines dependence on plausible alternatives. Changing a threshold, time grid, or mapping can alter the result without indicating a defect. The asset-criticality study's sensitivity analysis offers a precedent for examining such behavior \cite{assetcriticality2023}. For TDRP, reporting which services change class or relative position would help identify conclusions that rely heavily on particular settings. The purpose is to expose decision dependence, rather than demand that outputs remain stable under every substantive policy change.

\subsection{Usefulness and Transferability}
\label{sec:lr_validity}

Usefulness requires evidence from the people and tasks the artifact is intended to support. Practitioners could examine whether they can trace a priority to its decisive impact, explain a shared dependency's class, and identify unresolved assessment information. These tasks provide more specific evidence than overall satisfaction alone. The practitioner engagement in the self-assessment study and organizational feedback in the propagation study support the relevance of contextual evaluation \cite{selfassessment2025,raptaki2025propagation}.

Transferability depends on what is expected to transfer. The reviewed cases cover AMM information, container-terminal operations, physical assets, manufacturing recovery, and procurement processes \cite{amm2023bia,container2021bia,assetcriticality2023,sahebjamnia2015integrated,raptaki2025propagation}. This range supports the relevance of continuity analysis across settings. It does not validate one impact scale or set of deadlines for all of them. A transferable procedure should make adaptation requirements clear while preserving the meaning of its outputs under different approved configurations.

Evidence of operational effectiveness requires a stronger design than prototype testing. Reduced outage duration or incident loss depends on recovery capacity, staffing, procedures, disruption conditions, and actual use. Cases, scenarios, expert review, and usability exercises can support feasibility and bounded usefulness without isolating those operational effects. The evaluation of Masar should therefore distinguish demonstrated computational behavior and participant interpretation from benefits that would require deployment and longitudinal observation.

A proportionate evaluation for this master's project follows from these distinctions. The method can be assessed through its explicit semantics and independently reasoned cases. The implementation can be checked against those cases and configuration properties. Practitioner tasks can examine the interpretation of results and explanations. This combination aligns with the introduction's objectives while providing a credible basis for conclusions within the scope of a prototype.

\section{Comparative Synthesis and Research Gap}
\label{sec:lr_gap}

The literature connects continuity objectives to a sequence of related decisions. BIA identifies business consequences and recovery requirements. Criticality analysis classifies significance, prioritization establishes relative urgency, dependency analysis relates outcomes to enabling resources, and scheduling addresses execution under constraints. \input{tables/lr_synthesis}

The reviewed work establishes temporal assessment and configurable calculations. Horalek uses time-indexed BIA, government guidance connects impacts with recovery requirements, and commercial documentation describes parameter calculations \cite{amm2023bia,dga2025guidelineen,globalsuite2025prebia,servicenow2025rto}. Business-derived resource importance likewise has precedents in NIST guidance and asset-criticality research \cite{nist2018criticality,quinn2025bia,assetcriticality2023}. These contributions rule out a broad absence claim as the justification for Masar.

The project's research problem concerns a specific, inspectable transformation: service-level temporal assessments are interpreted through approved rules to produce criticality, MTPD, RTO, and relative recovery priorities, with an explicit relationship to supporting-dependency criticality. The reviewed sources inform this transformation but do not validate TDRP's particular combination of semantics, exceptional states, and inheritance policy. The contribution lies in specifying that combination coherently and evaluating the resulting artifact within the stated organizational purpose.

The synthesis identifies several requirements for this contribution. Temporal resolution and threshold interpretation must remain explicit. Recovery mappings must preserve the distinction between tolerability and a resumption target. Shared-dependency classification must have a defined ordering and aggregation policy. Results must retain sufficient assessment and configuration context for practitioners to understand how they were produced. These requirements follow from the comparison of existing approaches, rather than from an assumption that a new interface necessarily improves continuity practice.

The closest software precedents are particularly useful comparison points. ServiceNow and GlobalSuite demonstrate configurable temporal calculations, while GRASP and verinice connect assessments to dependencies and management workflows. TDRP should therefore be judged through the specificity of its method and evaluation, with any practical advantage over these systems left open to comparative investigation.

Masar realizes this bounded contribution as a web-based DSS presenting established assessments, recovery requirements, priorities, and linked dependencies. The methodology chapter can build on the review by defining valid configurations, specifying each derivation and exceptional state, and matching evaluation procedures to the claimed properties. The resulting evidence can support conclusions about correctness, consistency, traceability, and usefulness for selected tasks without extending them to unobserved incident outcomes.

\section{Chapter Summary}
\label{sec:lr_summary}

BCM provides the organizational context in which service recovery requirements are identified and governed. The reviewed literature shows how temporal BIA, multi-criteria methods, dependency analysis, and optimization address different parts of the recovery decision. Existing platforms already integrate important assessment and workflow capabilities. Their contributions provide a substantive basis for evaluating TDRP rather than treating the proposed application as an entirely new category of continuity software.

The review positions TDRP as a configurable service-level decision procedure linking temporal assessments, recovery requirements, priorities, and dependency classifications. Its evaluation must address methodological consistency, implementation correctness, and practitioner interpretation.
