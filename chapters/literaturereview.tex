\chapter{Literature Review}
\label{ch:literaturereview}

Business continuity decisions connect organizational obligations with the time available to sustain or restore essential services. This chapter reviews the concepts, analytical methods, and information systems that support those decisions. Its central concern is the transformation of business impact analysis (BIA) into defensible recovery requirements and relative service priorities, together with the interpretation of supporting dependencies. The review provides the basis for assessing the contribution of Time-Dependent Recovery Prioritization (TDRP) and its implementation in Masar, within the scope established in Chapter~\ref{ch:introduction}.

Section~\ref{sec:lr_bcm} establishes the management context and relevant guidance. Section~\ref{sec:lr_bia} examines impact assessment and recovery parameters. Section~\ref{sec:lr_prioritization} compares prioritization and scheduling approaches, and Section~\ref{sec:lr_dependencies} examines dependency models. Section~\ref{sec:lr_dss} reviews decision support and comparable software. Section~\ref{sec:lr_methods} evaluates the research methods used in related work. Section~\ref{sec:lr_gap} synthesizes the evidence and positions the research contribution, followed by the chapter summary in Section~\ref{sec:lr_summary}.

The review is a focused thematic synthesis rather than an exhaustive systematic review. Sources were selected for their relevance to continuity management, temporal BIA, service or asset criticality, dependencies, and decision-support evaluation. The evidence comprises research articles, government guidance, public standards descriptions, and product documentation. Foundational contributions are considered alongside recent applications; methodological relevance, rather than publication date alone, determines inclusion. Research findings are distinguished from normative guidance and advertised capabilities. Public ISO catalogue records support statements about scope, but not clause-level interpretations of the full standards. Product documentation establishes disclosed functions rather than independently measured effectiveness. These distinctions constrain the conclusions drawn from the review.

\section{Business Continuity Management}
\label{sec:lr_bcm}

\subsection{Continuity, Recovery, and Organizational Resilience}
\label{sec:lr_context}

Business continuity concerns the sustained delivery of prioritized products and services at an acceptable capacity when normal operations are disrupted. BCM organizes the responsibilities, assessments, plans, exercises, and improvement activities through which that capability is maintained. A business continuity management system (BCMS) formalizes these activities within an organizational management system. The scope of ISO 22301 places continuity within such a system rather than treating it as an isolated technical plan \cite{iso22301catalogue}. This distinction is consequential for information systems: an application may support selected BCM activities without constituting the organization's entire BCMS.

Continuity and disaster recovery overlap, but their decision objects differ. Continuity planning is concerned with the delivery of required business outcomes, potentially through temporary arrangements or reduced operating levels. Information-system recovery concerns the restoration of the technical resources that enable those outcomes. NIST SP 800-34 distinguishes information-system contingency planning from business continuity, crisis communications, incident response, and other related plans \cite{nist2010contingency}. A successfully restored server therefore does not, by itself, demonstrate that a business service has resumed at its required capacity. Personnel, data reconciliation, facilities, suppliers, and procedural prerequisites may remain unavailable.

Organizational resilience supplies a wider context. Sahebjamnia et al. connect continuity and recovery planning through the loss of operating capability over time and the resources needed to regain it \cite{sahebjamnia2015integrated}. Their analysis shows why short-term continuity and longer-term recovery should not be treated as independent demands on unlimited resources. However, resilience encompasses more than recovery speed. The asset-criticality framework of Aghabegloo et al., for example, considers absorptive, adaptive, and restorative capacities alongside sustainability criteria \cite{assetcriticality2023}. These broader perspectives help explain the significance of service recovery without implying that a prioritization method measures resilience in its entirety.

Risk assessment and BIA also answer related but different questions. Risk assessment examines uncertain events and their consequences; BIA establishes the significance of disrupted activities or assets in relation to organizational objectives. NIST IR 8286D connects BIA results to enterprise risk prioritization, distinguishing availability-related criticality from sensitivity associated with confidentiality and integrity \cite{quinn2025bia}. A service can have severe consequences if unavailable even when the probability of a particular initiating event is low. Combining likelihood and impact into a risk score should not silently replace the analysis of tolerable disruption duration.

\subsection{International Standards and Guidance}
\label{sec:lr_standards}

ISO 22301:2019 identifies requirements for establishing and maintaining a BCMS, while ISO 22313:2020 provides guidance on applying those requirements. ISO/TS 22317:2021 focuses on a documented BIA adapted to organizational needs, objectives, resources, and constraints \cite{iso22301catalogue,iso22313catalogue,iso22317catalogue}. The public description of ISO/TS 22317 explicitly allows different BIA processes. This adaptability supports contextual assessment, but it should not be interpreted as evidence that all computational procedures are equally appropriate. Organizations still need to justify the relationship between assessment criteria, tolerability, and recovery requirements.

\input{tables/lr_standards_comparison}

NIST SP 800-34 provides a more operational account for federal information systems. Its BIA identifies process criticality, supporting resources, and resource recovery priorities. Maximum tolerable downtime (MTD) expresses the outage a business process can accept, whereas RTO expresses the recovery target for a supporting system resource. RTO should normally be shorter than MTD because resumption may also require work recovery after technical restoration. RPO concerns the acceptable point to which data can be recovered; it is not an additional forward-running component of the outage budget \cite{nist2010contingency}. These definitions prevent a common conceptual error: treating every recovery-related time quantity as interchangeable.

The distinction between guidance and algorithm is equally important. NIST supplies a process, terminology, and examples through which an organization can establish requirements. Its illustrative durations do not constitute universal thresholds. Similarly, public ISO scope descriptions cannot establish that the complete standards either prescribe or omit a particular formula. The justified conclusion is narrower: the reviewed guidance supports organization-specific BIA and the explicit identification of recovery requirements, while the detailed computation used by a DSS remains an object requiring its own specification and assessment.

Research on BCM frameworks reinforces this need for interpretation. Russo et al. compare continuity-related frameworks and discuss the difficulty of operationalizing organizational maturity assessment \cite{russo2023maturity}. Arias-Aranda et al. identify a reference set of BCMS processes through standards analysis and practitioner verification \cite{bcms2026reference}. Such studies clarify the management activities within which a BIA tool operates. They do not demonstrate the correctness of a particular impact-to-RTO calculation. Agreement that BIA belongs in a BCMS is evidence about process relevance, not validation of every method implementing that process.

\subsection{Saudi National and Digital-Government Guidance}
\label{sec:lr_saudi}

The Saudi National Framework for Risk, Emergency and Business Continuity Management situates continuity within a broader organizational capability for managing disruption. Its BCM material connects essential services and products with enabling processes, impact assessment, recovery requirements, resources, and dependencies \cite{nrcnationalframework}. This service-oriented structure is directly relevant to the thesis: technical-resource importance is interpreted in relation to the organizational outcomes that resources enable.

The framework allows data collection through interviews, workshops, and questionnaires. Impact categories extend beyond direct financial loss to legal, operational, reputational, beneficiary, health and safety, and contractual consequences. Assessment times and classifications are adapted to the organization. Its example recovery classes illustrate how requirements may be expressed; they should not be adopted as universally mandatory cutoffs \cite{nrcnationalframework}. A configurable system should consequently separate the structure of an assessment from the particular parameter values approved by an organization.

The DGA guidance similarly relates BIA to an approved impact matrix, time-dependent disruption assessment, and the recovery requirements of digital government. Its outputs include MTPD, RTO, RPO, minimum business continuity objective (MBCO), and necessary resources \cite{dga2025bcm,dga2025guidelineen}. The assessment horizon depends on service sensitivity. The English issue also presents platform-impact tiers with associated target recovery times and adopts the highest level where impact factors produce different classifications \cite{dga2025guidelineen}. This is substantive prior art for explicit classification and mapping rules, even though it does not by itself settle every temporal interpretation needed by TDRP.

The national documents establish both a contextual basis and a constraint on the proposed contribution. Masar should accommodate approved organizational scales, but configurability is not permission to select arbitrary values without governance. A threshold represents a judgment about unacceptable consequences, and an RTO represents a target with operational implications. Their approval, ownership, and revision remain organizational responsibilities. A DSS can preserve and execute these decisions consistently; it cannot establish their appropriateness merely by producing a numerical result.

\section{Business Impact Analysis and Recovery Requirements}
\label{sec:lr_bia}

\subsection{Units of Analysis and Impact Evidence}
\label{sec:lr_bia_process}

The first methodological choice in BIA is the object being assessed. Studies variously examine products, business processes, services, datasets, systems, and physical assets. Horalek investigates automated meter management (AMM) data and supporting systems, while Budiyanto et al. examine the consequences of electricity disruption at a container terminal \cite{amm2023bia,container2021bia}. NIST starts from organizational processes and connects them to enabling resources \cite{nist2010contingency,quinn2025bia}. These objects are related, but a score assigned to a dataset cannot be assumed to have the same interpretation as a score assigned to an end-to-end public service.

A service-level assessment is useful when the decision concerns the restoration of outcomes delivered to beneficiaries. It can preserve the business rationale for subsequently identifying important resources. However, a service may contain activities with different deadlines, operate at several locations, or depend on alternative delivery channels. Defining service boundaries too broadly can conceal urgent components; defining them too narrowly can produce duplicate assessments and obscure shared obligations. The unit of analysis must therefore be stated before comparing criticality or recovery times across services.

Much BIA evidence is elicited from knowledgeable participants. Horalek combines technical analysis with guided interviews involving service sponsors and dataset owners \cite{amm2023bia}. Budiyanto et al. use management questionnaires and a focus group to establish relevant impacts and assessment scales, supplementing these judgments with operating records \cite{container2021bia}. Interviews can identify consequences that do not appear in transaction logs, including reputational damage and obligations to beneficiaries. Their limitations include inconsistent interpretations, incomplete knowledge of dependencies, and differences in how departments understand severe impact.

Operational data can strengthen such assessments without removing judgment. The container-terminal study relates disruption to productivity, throughput, working time, economic loss, and other consequences \cite{container2021bia}. Historical records help anchor the magnitude of observed effects, but a single outage does not reveal every future impact trajectory. A disruption at peak demand may differ from one during a quiet period, and a longer interruption can activate penalties or dependencies absent from the observed event. Data-supported BIA remains a combination of evidence, assumptions, and organizational interpretation.

Impact categories also require careful separation. Financial loss is often quantifiable, whereas reputation, beneficiary harm, legal exposure, and service quality may require ordinal judgments. NIST's distinction between criticality and sensitivity further shows why availability, confidentiality, and integrity should not be collapsed without an explicit purpose \cite{quinn2025bia}. An availability-focused recovery method may legitimately use several consequence categories, but it should not reinterpret all information-security assessments as downtime urgency. The source and meaning of each category should remain visible in the resulting decision record.

\subsection{Representing Impact over Time}
\label{sec:lr_time_impact}

A static impact label records severity without showing how quickly it arises. Temporal BIA instead evaluates interruption consequences at successive durations. The AMM study uses seven availability-assessment horizons, ranging from thirty minutes to more than one week \cite{amm2023bia}. This approach preserves an important difference between services that eventually experience similarly severe consequences but reach them at different times. For recovery prioritization, the onset of unacceptable impact may matter as much as the highest impact reached within the assessment horizon.

A time-indexed matrix is a practical representation of this evidence. Its rows or columns identify impact dimensions and assessment durations, and its cells record the corresponding ratings. The matrix remains an elicited or observed assessment, not a continuous physical law. A rating at eight hours does not reveal exactly what happened at every preceding minute. If an unacceptable rating is first observed at eight hours after an acceptable rating at four hours, locating the transition within that interval requires additional evidence or an explicit convention. Assigning eight hours as a policy boundary can be reproducible, but it should not be described as a measured crossing time.

Temporal resolution therefore creates a methodological trade-off. Closely spaced intervals can improve the usefulness of a matrix for sensitive services, but require more assessment effort and can suggest unjustified precision. Coarse intervals reduce elicitation demands while making threshold interpretation less exact. The DGA guidance recognizes variation in appropriate time scales across activities \cite{dga2025guidelineen}. A configurable method should preserve the declared horizon and time units, since identical ordinal ratings on different time grids do not necessarily represent equivalent urgency.

Time-dependent impact need not be uniformly increasing in every category. Cumulative financial loss may increase with duration, but deadline-based consequences can change abruptly, and temporary workarounds can reduce particular operational effects. The quantitative threat model QuantTM represents impact through recovery stages and time frames rather than a single static loss \cite{vonderassen2024quanttm}. Although its expected-loss purpose differs from service prioritization, it demonstrates why assumptions about persistence and recovery levels affect temporal interpretation. A method that requires monotone ratings must declare that requirement and identify inconsistent input rather than silently rearranging it.

Missing assessments create a separate problem. An empty cell may indicate insufficient information, an inapplicable category, or an assessment that has not yet been completed. None is necessarily equivalent to negligible impact. Likewise, failure to reach an unacceptable level within the observed horizon does not establish unlimited tolerability. These are logical consequences of working with incomplete, bounded assessments. They motivate explicit output states and explanations in a computational implementation, rather than the use of a convenient numerical default that could be mistaken for a confirmed recovery requirement.

\subsection{MTPD, RTO, and Related Parameters}
\label{sec:lr_parameters}

MTPD describes the tolerability boundary for disruption, while RTO expresses the target for resumption. The relationship between them depends on the assessed object and the activities required to restore acceptable service. NIST's distinction between process MTD and resource RTO makes this explicit: time may still be needed after system restoration to recover work and resume the process \cite{nist2010contingency}. Saudi guidance uses MTPD alongside RTO in the continuity assessment \cite{nrcnationalframework,dga2025guidelineen}. The terms can be compared conceptually, but calculations must preserve their original unit of analysis.

An RTO is therefore neither an observed recovery duration nor a guarantee of technical capability. It expresses a requirement or approved target that should inform recovery arrangements. A recovery exercise may demonstrate that existing arrangements fail to meet it. Conversely, a rapidly recoverable system may support a service whose tolerance is less demanding. A DSS should distinguish the target from demonstrated capability so that infeasible requirements remain visible as planning issues instead of being overwritten by current technical limitations.

MBCO adds a capacity dimension. Resuming a service at an acceptable reduced level is different from restoring its normal throughput. Sahebjamnia et al. represent operating levels and the requirements for maintaining or restoring them under resource constraints \cite{sahebjamnia2015integrated}. Their work illustrates why a time target without a specified operating condition can be ambiguous. For TDRP, the reviewed literature supports treating recovery requirements as contextual decisions: the relevant time, assessed service, and meaning of resumption should be recorded together even where capacity optimization lies outside the prototype.

RPO concerns tolerable data loss measured by reference to the point to which data must be recoverable \cite{nist2010contingency}. It should not be inferred solely from service downtime or treated as a second name for RTO. Terminology also needs checking when studies are compared. The planning model of Sahebjamnia et al. uses RPO in connection with a resumption operating level \cite{sahebjamnia2015integrated}; that usage is not adopted here. This difference is a reminder that shared abbreviations do not guarantee shared constructs across continuity research.

The AMM study relates time-based impacts to tolerable periods and proposes recovery objectives \cite{amm2023bia}. It is a close methodological precedent because the analysis links availability consequences across durations with recovery requirements. Its findings nevertheless remain grounded in one technical domain and its selected assessment criteria. A general-purpose service method requires a more explicit separation between the configurable policy, the calculation that executes it, and the evidence supporting the original ratings. That separation enables reproducibility without claiming that the ratings themselves are objective measurements.

\subsection{Comparative Evidence from BIA Studies}
\label{sec:lr_bia_approaches}

The analytical studies can be distinguished by what they produce. Matrix-based BIA supports impact classes and recovery requirements. Data-assisted BIA relates assessments to operating records. Multi-criteria models produce relative criticality scores; quantitative threat models estimate loss; process-mining models propagate relative incident effects; and optimization models allocate resources or determine recovery trajectories. Horalek's study is valuable for its explicit temporal matrix, but its evaluation is principally a worked assessment of an AMM environment \cite{amm2023bia}. Budiyanto et al. provide operational grounding for impact classification in a container-terminal disruption, yet the study does not establish a general impact-to-MTPD algorithm \cite{container2021bia}. Both show the importance of domain knowledge. Their contribution to a transferable DSS lies in the structure of assessment and the evidence-collection process, rather than in adopting their numerical scales without further justification.

Aghabegloo et al. extend the analytical scope by linking key products with physical-asset criticality under sustainability and resilience considerations \cite{assetcriticality2023}. Their method combines expert judgments and quantitative inputs through several decision techniques. Raptaki et al. instead derive a process graph from event logs and propagate incident-related impact across activities \cite{raptaki2025propagation}. These studies demonstrate that richer models can expose relationships missed by a simple matrix, while also increasing the requirements for elicitation, data preparation, and model interpretation.

\section{Service Criticality and Recovery Prioritization}
\label{sec:lr_prioritization}

\subsection{Multi-Criteria Approaches}
\label{sec:lr_mcdm}

Criticality assessment usually involves several consequences that cannot be expressed in one natural unit. Multi-criteria decision-making methods offer an explicit means of combining these judgments. Aghabegloo et al. use fuzzy best--worst weighting and fuzzy TOPSIS to identify key products, followed by fuzzy DEMATEL and a Bayesian network to evaluate supporting physical assets \cite{assetcriticality2023}. The approach recognizes that economic, social, environmental, and resilience factors may influence one another rather than contributing as isolated criteria.

The gas-plant application illustrates both the power and demands of this approach. Product selection precedes asset assessment, retaining a business rationale for the asset priorities. Expert judgments, historical information, and risk scenarios inform the model. Its asset criticality index combines weighted probabilities associated with critical factors, and further analysis identifies influential contributors \cite{assetcriticality2023}. The result provides a basis for directing attention and continuity investment. It does not directly supply a service's tolerable interruption duration from successive ordinal ratings.

Weighted aggregation requires a defensible interpretation of the scales and trade-offs. An average can allow low values in several dimensions to offset an unacceptable value in another. That may be appropriate when criteria are compensatory, but inappropriate when a single legal or safety consequence is decisive. Fuzzy methods represent imprecision in judgments; they do not eliminate the need to justify which judgments can compensate for others. Likewise, a probabilistic index is meaningful only in relation to its variables, elicitation assumptions, and network structure.

The criteria dependencies in Aghabegloo et al. also differ from service-resource dependencies. Their Bayesian network represents relationships among factors affecting asset criticality \cite{assetcriticality2023}. It should not be described as equivalent to recording which services use a shared database. The former models how criteria interact in assessment; the latter models operational support relationships. Confusing these objects would obscure both the strengths of the multi-criteria approach and the narrower purpose of Masar's dependency map.

Multi-criteria methods are therefore relevant alternatives when decision makers need explicit trade-offs across several dimensions and can support the necessary elicitation. For TDRP, their principal lesson is that aggregation semantics must be transparent. A simpler rule is not inherently more valid, but may be more readily inspected when the intended policy is a categorical tolerability threshold rather than a compensatory utility score. Comparative evaluation should ask whether the method represents the organization's decision policy correctly, rather than assuming that greater mathematical complexity implies better prioritization.

\subsection{Configurable Rule-Based Approaches}
\label{sec:lr_rules}

Rule-based approaches apply declared conditions and mappings to assessed impacts. A rule may select a criticality category, identify a tolerability boundary, or map an assessed class to an approved recovery target. The DGA's highest-impact classification is an example of a non-compensatory policy: a severe consequence is not diluted by lower ratings elsewhere \cite{dga2025guidelineen}. This approach is intelligible to practitioners because the decisive category can be identified, but its appropriateness still depends on the definition of the impact scale and the intended use of the result.

Commercial documentation provides closer precedents for temporal rules. GlobalSuite records impacts over configurable time scales and documents automatic MTPD criteria based on a selected impact level, an average, or a specific impact type. It also supports consolidation across individual assessments through average, weighted-average, maximum, or minimum rules \cite{globalsuite2025prebia}. ServiceNow documents RTO calculations based on impact tolerability and disruption durations, with category results combined through a minimum and a configured fallback when no intolerable rating occurs \cite{servicenow2025rto}. These examples preclude a claim that configurable temporal BIA calculation is new in itself.

The analytical issue is the meaning of each rule under valid and exceptional inputs. Selecting the earliest threshold breach differs from selecting a rating according to its code order. Taking a maximum across dimensions differs from averaging their ratings. Choosing a default duration when no threshold is reached differs from reporting that tolerability has not been established within the horizon. Each procedure can be deterministic, yet yield a different interpretation. Reproducibility consequently requires more than documenting a formula for the normal case.

A complete specification must also address equality at a threshold, missing categories, irregularly spaced periods, invalid configuration, and tied priorities. Where two services share the same decision-relevant attributes, preserving an equal priority may be more defensible than inventing a substantive distinction. If a stable display order is required, the display tie-breaker should not be mistaken for an additional continuity judgment. These requirements arise from the formalization of decision rules; they are not evidence that existing products necessarily lack corresponding safeguards.

Configurability introduces a governance problem as well as a technical capability. The same assessment can produce different outputs under different approved configurations. A traceable record must therefore connect the result to both the assessment and the rule version. It should also distinguish a recalculated result from a historical approval. NIST's emphasis on recording BIA values and their rationale supports this broader view of traceability \cite{quinn2025bia}. For Masar, the relevant research opportunity is to make this chain explicit and testable within the proposed service-prioritization procedure.

\subsection{Priority Rankings and Recovery Schedules}
\label{sec:lr_sequencing}

A priority ranking identifies relative importance or urgency. A recovery schedule assigns actions, resources, and start or completion times while respecting operational constraints. NIST's contingency-planning guidance already recognizes technical prerequisites in restoration \cite{nist2010contingency}. A high-priority application may depend on an infrastructure service that must be restored first. Recovering the infrastructure first does not mean that its independent business impact exceeds the application's; it means that restoration is constrained by dependencies.

Sahebjamnia et al. formulate integrated continuity and disaster recovery planning as a multi-objective mixed-integer programming problem. BIA supplies tolerability and minimum operating requirements, while the model considers resource capacities, budgets, operating levels, and recovery over discrete periods \cite{sahebjamnia2015integrated}. Their manufacturing case demonstrates trade-offs between maintaining capability and restoring operations. The model addresses how to allocate resources under specified assumptions, rather than merely deriving service urgency from impact ratings.

El-Shekeil et al.'s PRECESION model examines progressive restoration in enterprise data centers. It represents user requests, services, servers, their dependencies, and limited human recovery capacity. A metaheuristic searches for restoration sequences that improve weighted availability over the recovery horizon \cite{elshekeil2017precesion}. Importance weights are inputs to that scheduling problem. The model thus provides a concrete example of why deriving business priority and executing a feasible restoration sequence are distinct analytical stages.

The distinction also limits comparative claims. An optimization model may produce a better schedule according to its objective function while relying on priorities that were determined elsewhere. A rule-based BIA method may improve consistency in those priorities without optimizing the subsequent schedule. Neither result demonstrates superiority over the other for all continuity decisions. TDRP's outputs can inform planning, but they should not be presented as instructions that automatically account for repair duration, staffing, technical precedence, or changing incident conditions.

\section{Dependency Modeling and Supporting-Resource Criticality}
\label{sec:lr_dependencies}

\subsection{Service and Resource Relationships}
\label{sec:lr_service_dependency}

Services are delivered through combinations of activities, information, technology, facilities, personnel, and external providers. NIST's BIA process connects business processes to required resources, and the Saudi frameworks include dependencies within continuity assessment \cite{nist2010contingency,nrcnationalframework,dga2025guidelineen}. A service-first model records these relationships so that resource importance can be explained through supported outcomes. This is especially important for shared resources whose significance is not apparent from their individual technical descriptions.

A dependency statement needs an unambiguous direction and meaning. A relationship indicating that service A requires resource B is different from one indicating that A merely exchanges information with B. A required resource may have substitutes, a manual workaround, or a minimum capacity below normal operating capacity. Consequently, recording an association is an initial representation of dependence, not a complete causal model of service failure. The interpretation adopted by a DSS should remain proportionate to the information actually collected.

PRECESION illustrates a more detailed representation through dependencies between user requests, services, and servers, including relationships within layers \cite{elshekeil2017precesion}. That structure is suitable for restoration planning because the model needs to determine when services become available as servers recover. A simpler service-resource map can still support criticality explanation, but cannot reproduce the same scheduling behavior unless it also contains the required availability conditions and recovery constraints.

Shared dependencies create an additional aggregation issue. If several services rely on one resource, its importance is related to all of them, not just to the first service entered into the system. Conversely, counting the number of supported services alone may undervalue a resource that enables one exceptionally time-sensitive service. The appropriate rule depends on the decision purpose: identifying the strongest inherited requirement, measuring aggregate exposure, and allocating recovery resources are different tasks. A single score should not be assumed to answer all three.

\subsection{Interdependencies and Impact Propagation}
\label{sec:lr_interdependency}

Rinaldi et al. distinguish physical, cyber, geographic, and logical interdependencies among critical infrastructures \cite{rinaldi2001interdependencies}. Their conceptual framework extends dependency analysis beyond direct technical links. Two services can share exposure because they occupy the same location, depend on the same external network, or are connected through organizational rules. These relationships help explain how disruption can propagate or affect apparently separate systems simultaneously.

The framework is useful for structuring inquiry, but it does not automatically generate a quantitative propagation model. Such a model requires information about strength, timing, operating states, and possible substitutions. Geographic co-location, for example, indicates common exposure without proving that the failure of one service causes the failure of another. This distinction matters when dependency diagrams are interpreted on dashboards: connectivity is evidence of a relationship, not necessarily a probability or magnitude of cascading loss.

Raptaki et al. offer an empirical approach to deriving process relationships from event logs. Their method discovers a process graph, assigns transition weights, and propagates a selected incident's relative impact through that graph. A Java application uses process-mining components and exports rankings and graph data. Evaluation uses procurement records from one international transportation company together with organizational assessments and interviews \cite{raptaki2025propagation}. This approach can reveal differences between documented processes and enacted activity.

Its strengths also define its limits. Logged activity supports the observed process model; unlogged work and relationships outside the selected process may remain invisible. Propagated impact values depend on the chosen incident point, graph, and weighting procedure. They are not automatically failure probabilities, service MTPD values, or absolute business-loss estimates. The authors' scenario-sensitive rankings therefore address a different question from TDRP's interpretation of approved service-level temporal assessments \cite{raptaki2025propagation}.

Together, these studies suggest a progression from descriptive dependency inventories to analytical propagation and scheduling models. The appropriate level depends on the research question and available evidence. Masar's stated scope supports linking services with enabling dependencies and explaining inherited criticality. Extending it to infer cascading incidents would require additional modeling and validation beyond what a dependency map alone can establish.

\subsection{Assigning Criticality to Shared Dependencies}
\label{sec:lr_criticality}

NIST IR 8179 analyzes criticality across programs, systems, and components, with attention to relationships among levels and shared components \cite{nist2018criticality}. NIST IR 8286D similarly derives the significance of assets from their contribution to enterprise objectives \cite{quinn2025bia}. These sources establish a clear precedent for business-derived resource criticality. The proposed use of service assessments to explain dependency importance should therefore be positioned as a specific operationalization of an existing principle.

The highest-supported-service rule proposed in the introduction has a comprehensible purpose: a shared dependency should retain the strongest criticality class among the services it supports. This avoids averaging away a highly critical service's requirement. However, this rule is a project policy to be evaluated, not a universal conclusion of the cited guidance. It captures the strongest inherited class but does not distinguish a resource supporting one critical service from another supporting many equally critical services.

The rule also presupposes a common ordering of criticality classes. Numerical codes are insufficient unless their ordering is explicit: in some scales, a smaller code indicates greater criticality. Missing service assessments should not be converted into a low class, and a dependency with no recorded services should not be interpreted as demonstrably unimportant. These cases show why implementation correctness must be evaluated against the semantic definition of the rule rather than against a convenient database maximum operation.

Criticality inheritance and recovery-time assignment should remain separate. A dependency may support several services with different RTOs, and its restoration may need to precede service resumption by a preparation interval. Inheriting the highest class does not determine that interval or establish a feasible dependency recovery target. The literature on contingency planning and scheduling supports maintaining this distinction \cite{nist2010contingency,elshekeil2017precesion}. For TDRP, inherited criticality is an explanatory output connecting resources to business requirements, not a substitute for operational recovery engineering.

\section{Decision Support Systems and Comparable Applications}
\label{sec:lr_dss}

\subsection{Decision Support Foundations}
\label{sec:lr_dss_foundations}

Decision support systems organize information and analytical capabilities to assist judgment. Power's historical account describes several streams of DSS development, while Arnott et al.'s analysis of DSS research identifies a broad range of systems and methods \cite{power2007history,arnott2005descriptive}. A continuity DSS can combine structured records, analytical rules, and interactive presentation without becoming an autonomous decision maker. Its value depends on the connection between those capabilities and the decisions of identifiable users.

Arnott et al. also raise concerns about professional relevance and insufficient identification of clients and users in the research they examined \cite{arnott2005descriptive}. Their sample covers 1990--2003 and should not be treated as a description of all contemporary DSS research. Nevertheless, the methodological implication remains pertinent: an artifact should state who uses it, what decision it supports, and how improvement is assessed. For Masar, continuity practitioners maintain and interpret service assessments, while authorized decision makers retain responsibility for operational priorities and recovery actions.

Three functions follow from this purpose. The data function preserves services, assessments, configurations, and dependencies. The analytical function applies declared rules consistently. The presentation function exposes results and their rationale in a form practitioners can examine. These are complementary responsibilities. A dashboard that displays a priority without showing its assessment basis may improve visibility while leaving the decision opaque; a correct rule engine without usable explanations may be difficult to challenge or apply appropriately.

Transparency also requires separating recorded evidence from derived results. Users should be able to distinguish an entered impact rating, an approved threshold, a calculated MTPD, and an assigned priority. NIST's BIA register provides a related emphasis on recording values and rationale for communication across organizational levels \cite{quinn2025bia}. The information-system contribution lies partly in preserving these relationships through revision, so that a result remains interpretable after services, assessments, or policies change.

\subsection{Research Prototypes and Analytical Applications}
\label{sec:lr_dss_domain}

Research applications demonstrate different forms of continuity-related support. QuantTM presents a browser-based approach to quantifying cyber-threat consequences through business impact and recovery stages \cite{vonderassen2024quanttm}. Its output is expected financial loss under threat assumptions. This can support risk discussion, but it differs from assigning service priority from an approved temporal impact matrix. The work is a preprint, and its evidence should be weighed accordingly rather than treated as equivalent to independently replicated operational results.

Raptaki et al.'s application supports process discovery and impact propagation, connecting event data to scenario-sensitive activity rankings \cite{raptaki2025propagation}. Its implementation is important because it demonstrates how analytical models can be integrated with practical data processing and export. However, the quality of its output depends on event-log coverage and interpretation. Automation can reduce some manual modeling effort while leaving substantive organizational judgments unresolved.

Russo et al.'s self-assessment tool addresses BCM preparedness through structured questions, weighted scoring, explanatory guidance, and visual results \cite{selfassessment2025}. The study includes practitioner engagement and organizational interviews, providing evidence about interpretation and perceived usefulness. Its decision target is organizational improvement, not temporal service criticality. A preparedness score cannot be relabeled as a recovery priority, and anticipated recovery benefits should not be reported as measured incident outcomes.

ServiceNow's BIA documentation describes structured assessment workflows, while its recovery-objective documentation specifies calculations using tolerability, disruption durations, and configured mappings \cite{servicenow2025bia,servicenow2025rto}. GRASP GRC documents impact assessment across time horizons, recovery objectives, linked assets and providers, and the recording of rationale \cite{grasp2025bia}. The software comparison therefore changes the formulation of the research gap. Masar cannot be justified simply by claiming that existing tools do not support BIA, time-dependent assessment, dependencies, or dashboards. The defensible objective is to specify and evaluate a bounded, transparent method whose assessment inputs, rule configuration, recovery outputs, relative priorities, and dependency classifications can be examined together. Whether this offers practical advantages over existing tools requires comparative evidence and cannot be assumed from the prototype's proposed functionality.

\section{Research Methods and Evaluation Evidence}
\label{sec:lr_methods}

\subsection{Design Science and Artifact Development}
\label{sec:lr_design_science}

Design-science research is appropriate when the research contribution includes a purposeful artifact addressing an organizational problem. Hevner et al. emphasize the relationship among relevance, rigor, artifact construction, and evaluation \cite{hevner2004design}. Peffers et al. articulate activities covering problem identification, solution objectives, design and development, demonstration, evaluation, and communication \cite{peffers2007dsrm}. These perspectives explain why developing Masar is necessary but not sufficient: implementation must be connected to an explicit research problem and supported by evidence about the resulting artifact.

TDRP and Masar are analytically distinguishable artifacts. TDRP specifies the decision procedure, while Masar realizes it in software. A defect in a threshold rule is different from a programming error in executing a correct rule. Similarly, an accurate computation may still be presented in a way that users misunderstand. Separating these layers makes it possible to evaluate methodological consistency, implementation correctness, and practical interpretation without treating them as one undifferentiated claim of system quality.

The related studies use several development strategies. Sahebjamnia et al. formalize a planning model and examine its application in a manufacturing setting \cite{sahebjamnia2015integrated}. Raptaki et al. combine analytical development with process data and organizational feedback \cite{raptaki2025propagation}. Russo et al. develop and refine a self-assessment artifact through expert and organizational engagement \cite{selfassessment2025}. These approaches offer complementary precedents, but none prescribes the exact evaluation design for TDRP. The evaluation must follow the properties the thesis claims to establish.

\subsection{Correctness, Usefulness, and Operational Effectiveness}
\label{sec:lr_evaluation}

Venable et al.'s Framework for Evaluation in Design Science (FEDS) distinguishes formative from summative evaluation and artificial from naturalistic evaluation settings \cite{venable2016feds}. Formative evaluation informs improvement; summative evaluation judges the artifact against expectations. Artificial settings permit controlled examination of selected properties, whereas naturalistic settings examine use in real organizational contexts. These dimensions should not be reduced to a simple sequence in which every early test is formative and every later interview is proof of effectiveness.

For a deterministic method, correctness can be examined through independently specified cases with known expected outcomes. Relevant cases include threshold equality, an impact already unacceptable at the first period, no observed threshold crossing, incomplete assessments, tied priorities, and several services sharing a dependency. Expected results must come from the declared semantics rather than being copied from the implementation being tested. Otherwise, the same error may appear in both calculation and reference output. These are implications of the artifact's claims, not evaluations already completed in the present thesis.

Consistency concerns how results behave across repeated execution and controlled input changes. Repeating an assessment under the same configuration should reproduce its result. Reordering stored records should not alter a priority unless order is explicitly part of the policy. If higher severity is intended never to reduce criticality, that property should be checked over valid configurations. Such tests assess the internal consequences of the chosen method; they do not establish that the organization's original thresholds reflect actual harm accurately.

Sensitivity analysis addresses a different question: how strongly conclusions depend on plausible changes in assumptions. The asset-criticality study uses sensitivity analysis alongside expert face validation \cite{assetcriticality2023}. For TDRP, changing approved thresholds or time grids could alter classifications and rankings even when the implementation is correct. Reporting these changes would help distinguish robust conclusions from priorities that depend heavily on a particular configuration. Stability is not always desirable, however: an appropriately responsive method should change its output when a materially different tolerability judgment is introduced.

Usefulness and interpretability require human evidence. Practitioners may be asked to explain a displayed priority, identify its decisive impact, trace a shared dependency's class, or recognize an incomplete assessment. Successful completion supports claims about particular tasks and users. General satisfaction alone is weaker evidence that the decision rationale was understood. The practitioner-oriented evaluation in the self-assessment study and the organizational feedback in the process-mining study illustrate the value of contextual engagement, while retaining the limits of their respective samples \cite{selfassessment2025,raptaki2025propagation}.

Operational effectiveness requires stronger evidence still. A prototype demonstration cannot establish reduced outage duration, lower incident losses, or improved organizational resilience. Those outcomes depend on staffing, infrastructure, procedures, disruption characteristics, and actual use of the system. The literature reviewed here frequently combines cases, scenarios, expert assessment, and sensitivity analysis. It supports feasibility and contextual usefulness more directly than universal causal claims about recovery performance. The evaluation of Masar should preserve this distinction when interpreting its own results.

\subsection{Transferability and Evidence Quality}
\label{sec:lr_validity}

Single-case studies provide detailed context but limited grounds for generalization across sectors. The AMM, container-terminal, gas-plant, manufacturing, and procurement studies address materially different operating environments \cite{amm2023bia,container2021bia,assetcriticality2023,sahebjamnia2015integrated,raptaki2025propagation}. Their diversity supports the general relevance of continuity analysis, yet does not establish that one shared numerical scale is valid across them. Transferability should concern the reasoning structure and adaptation requirements, with local parameters justified separately.

For this thesis, the literature supports a proportionate evaluation: explicit rule specification, independently checked cases, tests of configuration behavior and shared dependencies, and practitioner examination of traceability and interpretation. Claims about organizational benefits should be limited to the evidence actually obtained. This provides a coherent route from the introductory objectives to a design-science contribution without requiring a prototype study to demonstrate effects that only deployment and longitudinal observation could establish.

\section{Comparative Synthesis and Research Gap}
\label{sec:lr_gap}

The reviewed literature establishes a connected chain of decisions. BCM defines the organizational need to sustain prioritized outcomes. BIA identifies the consequences of interruption and their temporal significance. Prioritization methods translate judgments into classes or relative rankings. Dependency models connect those outcomes to enabling resources, while planning and scheduling methods consider how restoration can be executed. \input{tables/lr_synthesis}

Several capabilities are already established. Standards and government guidance support contextual BIA, recovery requirements, and dependency identification. Research demonstrates temporal impact matrices, business-derived asset criticality, mathematical recovery planning, and process-based propagation. Product documentation describes configurable temporal calculations and integrated continuity workflows \cite{nist2010contingency,amm2023bia,assetcriticality2023,sahebjamnia2015integrated,raptaki2025propagation,globalsuite2025prebia,servicenow2025rto}. A broad claim that prior work lacks time sensitivity, decision rules, or service-resource relationships would therefore be unsupported.

The remaining research problem is more specific. The thesis seeks a fully stated, configurable procedure that connects service-level temporal assessments to criticality, MTPD, RTO, and relative priorities, then explains the criticality assigned to supporting dependencies. The reviewed sources provide important parts of this procedure, but do not collectively validate the exact combination and interpretation proposed for TDRP. Its contribution must be demonstrated through the coherence of that specification, its treatment of exceptional inputs, and the evaluation of its implementation and explanations.

This gap is an artifact-specific design and evaluation problem, not proof of an unoccupied software market. GlobalSuite and ServiceNow are particularly close prior art for temporal rule execution. NIST supplies prior art for business-derived criticality, and the research on scheduling establishes a clear boundary on what a ranking can claim. These precedents narrow the novelty claim while improving the basis for design. They provide concepts and comparison points against which TDRP can be examined rather than reasons to disregard the proposed project.

Three requirements emerge from the synthesis. First, temporal interpretation must be explicit: ratings, time horizons, threshold semantics, and recovery mappings must retain distinct meanings. Second, the chain from input to output must be traceable, including configuration and shared-dependency rules. Third, evaluation must distinguish correct execution from appropriate policy and practical usefulness. These requirements align with the introduction's emphasis on correctness, consistency, and traceability, while leaving real-incident effectiveness outside what a prototype alone can establish.

Masar operationalizes this bounded contribution as a web-based DSS. Its intended dashboard presents approved assessment results and their context; it does not infer recovery procedures or execute operational decisions. The methodology chapter can consequently build on a precise literature-derived foundation: formalize the proposed rules, justify their assumptions, describe their implementation, and establish evaluation criteria corresponding to each claimed property.

\section{Chapter Summary}
\label{sec:lr_summary}

The literature identifies BIA as a central connection between organizational continuity objectives and recovery requirements. Time-dependent assessment distinguishes services by the onset of unacceptable consequences, while criticality and dependency analysis explain why enabling resources matter. Research offers matrix-based, multi-criteria, probabilistic, process-mining, and optimization approaches, each with different inputs and decision outputs. Comparable software already supports important elements of temporal assessment, configured calculations, and continuity workflows.

The review supports a bounded contribution for TDRP: an explicit and evaluable procedure linking service assessments, recovery requirements, relative priorities, and supporting-dependency criticality. It also establishes the limits of that contribution. A reproducible priority is not a guaranteed appropriate policy, a dependency class is not a restoration schedule, and a working prototype is not evidence of improved recovery during actual disruptions. These distinctions provide the basis for the design and evaluation of Masar in the next chapter.
