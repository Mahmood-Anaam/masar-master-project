\chapter{Introduction}
\label{ch:introduction}

\section{Motivation and Scope}
\label{sec:motivation}

Organizations deliver their services through interconnected applications, facilities, personnel, suppliers, and records. Because these elements depend on one another, a disruption that affects one of them can spread to the others and impair several business services at once. Continuity teams must then decide which services to restore first, how long each service can remain unavailable, and which supporting resources have to be recovered before a service can resume. Such decisions carry legal, financial, and operational consequences, and they are usually taken under time pressure and with incomplete information.

Business continuity management (BCM) is the discipline through which organizations prepare for disruptions, respond to them, and continue delivering prioritized products and services at an acceptable level. ISO 22301 specifies the requirements for establishing, implementing, maintaining, and continually improving a business continuity management system. Within this discipline, business impact analysis (BIA) is a central analytical activity, since it examines the consequences of an interruption and provides the basis on which recovery requirements are established \cite{quinn2022bia}. Four such requirements are commonly distinguished, of which the first two are the focus of this project. The maximum tolerable period of disruption (MTPD) is the longest period during which a service can remain disrupted before the resulting consequences become unacceptable, whereas the recovery time objective (RTO) is the target time within which a service or a supporting resource should be restored. The recovery point objective (RPO) expresses the maximum acceptable amount of data loss measured in time, and the minimum business continuity objective (MBCO) defines the lowest level of products or services that must be maintained during a disruption. To support decisions, each requirement needs a clearly defined scope, unit of measurement, approval status, and period of validity, and its relationship to the affected service or resource must be explicit.

The impact of a disruption is not constant. A service that tolerates a short interruption may become increasingly critical as the interruption continues and its operational, financial, or regulatory consequences accumulate. Business impact analysis therefore examines how impacts develop over time, and the resulting time-dependent impact profile offers a basis for deriving recovery requirements and for understanding how the urgency of restoring a service changes during a disruption \cite{quinn2022bia}. These requirements are set before an incident occurs, yet they guide the decisions made while it unfolds. The quality of those decisions therefore depends on how the requirements are derived, and on whether the reasoning behind them can be reproduced, examined, and justified.

Information systems can support this process by organizing relevant information, assisting analysis, and supporting recovery planning and decision-making. Earlier studies have explored several directions, including data mining techniques \cite{idrees2019bcis}, estimation of recovery effort \cite{podaras2022regression}, process mining \cite{raptaki2025impact}, and the optimization of restoration decisions \cite{shahverdi2025recovery}. This project addresses a complementary question: how recovery requirements established before an incident can be connected to the prioritization decisions required during an actual disruption. Its scope covers the design of a rule-based methodology for deriving MTPD and RTO from time-dependent impact information and for prioritizing recovery according to service criticality and dependencies, together with a decision support system that applies the methodology and presents its results. The system is intended to support decision-making and does not replace the judgment of authorized personnel. It does not generate recovery recommendations, simulate disruption scenarios, or take operational decisions.

\section{Problem Statement and Significance for Information Systems}
\label{sec:problem}

During a disruption, continuity teams have to determine the recovery requirements of the affected services, understand their criticality and dependencies, and establish appropriate recovery priorities. Organizations may well hold the necessary material in the form of business impact analysis results, recovery plans, technical information, and supporting records. However, this material is often distributed across multiple documents and files, so that locating, reviewing, and connecting it during an incident demands considerable manual effort. Collecting information in this way can delay the moment at which priorities are established, at a time when rapid action is essential.

A second difficulty concerns the recovery requirements themselves. Determining them requires a consistent way of translating the time-dependent impact of a disruption into defined recovery objectives, because otherwise the resulting values depend heavily on who performs the analysis and cannot easily be examined afterwards. Recovery priorities add a further layer of complexity, since they must account for the criticality of each service and for the dependencies between services and the technical systems that support them. A service cannot be resumed before the resources on which it relies are available, so priorities set without regard to these relationships may prove unworkable in practice.

The problem addressed by this project can therefore be stated as the lack of an integrated business continuity system that applies a structured, rule-based methodology to determine recovery requirements and priorities from time-dependent impact information, service criticality, and dependencies, while offering a consolidated view of the resulting information.

The problem is significant for the information systems field for several reasons. It concerns the use of an information system to organize dispersed organizational knowledge and to turn it into usable decision support, a theme that has long been central to research on decision support systems \cite{arnott2005critical}. It also calls for the construction and evaluation of a purposeful artifact, which is the type of contribution that design-oriented information systems research seeks to provide \cite{hevner2004design}. Finally, the decisions at stake are consequential, and in such settings the use of models whose reasoning can be inspected has been argued to be preferable to opaque ones \cite{rudin2019stop}. A methodology whose rules are stated explicitly therefore addresses both a practical need of continuity teams and a recognized concern of the field.

\section{Importance of the Problem and the Big Picture}
\label{sec:importance}

The importance of the problem lies in what follows from recovery decisions. If the time targets assigned to services or the order of restoration are poorly founded, an organization may restore less critical services before critical ones, or fail to meet its legal and operational obligations. Time spent searching for documents is also time during which the impact of the disruption keeps growing, so delays in reaching supporting information add directly to the cost of an incident.

Viewed more broadly, the problem lies where two stages of business continuity management meet: the analysis performed in advance, which produces recovery requirements before any disruption occurs \cite{quinn2022bia}, and the decisions taken later, under pressure, on the basis of whatever information can be reached. Linking these stages through a methodology that is explicit, repeatable, and traceable allows the reasoning behind a priority to be reviewed and justified, and gives continuity teams a consistent starting point instead of an ad hoc one. Placing that methodology in a single system that also holds the relevant continuity information shortens the path from analysis to action, while accountability for the final decision stays with the responsible personnel. The contribution is therefore relevant to any organization that depends on interconnected services and must be able to explain how its recovery priorities were established.

\section{Objectives and Proposed Solution}
\label{sec:objectives}

The overall aim of this project is to propose a rule-based methodology for deriving recovery requirements and recovery priorities from time-dependent impact information, and to present it within an integrated system that supports continuity teams during a disruption. To this end, the project defines the methodology and its decision rules, specifies a structured repository of the information on which the rules operate, and designs a dashboard that presents the resulting requirements and priorities together with the information behind them. The methodology is the primary contribution, and the system serves as the means through which it is applied and made accessible.

The proposed solution is Masar, an integrated web-based business continuity system that incorporates a rule-based methodology called Time-Dependent Recovery Prioritization (TDRP). The methodology uses a predefined impact matrix and a set of decision rules to evaluate the impact of a disruption across successive time frames. The MTPD is assigned according to the time frame in which the impact first reaches the defined high-impact threshold, and the corresponding RTO is determined by the rules associated with that time frame. The specific time frames, impact thresholds, and recovery rules will be specified and documented as part of the methodology design. Once the recovery requirements have been determined, the methodology establishes recovery priorities by applying its rules to service criticality, recovery requirements, and the dependencies between services and their supporting technical systems.

Masar maintains a structured repository of business continuity information, including services, business impact analysis results, time-dependent impact assessments, service criticality, recovery requirements, dependencies, technical resources, recovery procedures, and relevant supporting records. The rule-based processes draw on this repository, and an operational dashboard presents their results, namely the recovery requirements, service priorities, criticality, and dependencies. By bringing the methodology, the information, and the presentation of results together, Masar allows continuity teams to reach the results and the information behind them in one place, instead of retrieving and connecting it manually from multiple documents and files.

A rule-based approach was selected because its decision logic can be defined explicitly and then reviewed and validated by business continuity professionals. Given the same input data and rule configuration, the methodology is designed to produce the same results, which supports transparency and traceability and keeps the approach feasible within the scope of the project. Operational recovery decisions remain the responsibility of authorized personnel.
