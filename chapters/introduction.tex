\chapter{Introduction}
\label{ch:introduction}

\section{Motivation and Scope}
\label{sec:motivation-scope}

Organizations depend on business services delivered through combinations of people, information, applications, facilities, and external providers. Restoring an application after a disruption may therefore be insufficient to resume the service it supports. Business continuity management (BCM) addresses this wider concern by establishing arrangements for continuing prioritized activities and recovering disrupted operations. Gibb and Buchanan situate BCM within an organization's information strategy, connecting continuity arrangements with the management of operational change and disruption \cite{gibb2006framework}. This perspective motivates the present project's focus on the relationship between business recovery needs and the information required to interpret them.

Business impact analysis (BIA) provides an analytical basis for establishing these needs. It examines the consequences of interruption and helps identify activities requiring priority. Torabi et al. address critical functions and continuity measures, including the maximum tolerable period of disruption (MTPD) and the minimum business continuity objective (MBCO) \cite{torabi2014framework}. In this project, MTPD denotes the duration beyond which interruption of a specified service produces unacceptable consequences; MBCO describes the minimum acceptable service level during disruption. Assessing consequences at successive elapsed-time intervals produces a time-dependent impact profile, making explicit how the significance of an interruption changes with its duration.

Recovery requirements also distinguish restoration time from data recovery. NIST contingency planning guidance differentiates tolerable business-process downtime, the recovery time objective (RTO) for system resources, and the recovery point objective (RPO) for data \cite{swanson2010contingency}. Masar uses RTO for the target duration within which a specified service or resource should be restored to its required level, and RPO for the point to which its data should be recovered. Each requirement must retain its scope: a business service and its supporting application have related but distinct recovery needs. An RTO must also be assessed against available recovery capability before it can serve as an approved planning target.

These assessments are useful only when their supporting information can be brought together. For example, a continuity coordinator reviewing an interrupted service may need its impact profile, approved recovery target, supporting systems, and recovery procedures. Where these records are maintained separately, their identifiers, versions, and assumptions must be reconciled before priorities can be interpreted. This project addresses this information management problem without assuming that its prevalence or operational cost has already been measured in a particular organization.

The proposed response is \emph{Masar}, an integrated web-based decision support system incorporating a rule-based method called \emph{Time-Dependent Recovery Prioritization} (TDRP). It will connect impact profiles with candidate recovery requirements and relative service priorities, supported by a shared repository and dashboard. Its intended users are continuity coordinators, service owners, and technical recovery personnel within one organization. The scope covers service and resource records, impact assessments, recovery requirements, dependencies, and associated procedures. It excludes autonomous recovery execution, predictive incident detection, disruption simulation, and optimization of resource-constrained recovery schedules. The current IS595 work establishes the academic foundation and conceptual proposal; implementation, testing, and evaluation belong to the subsequent IS597 project.

\section{Problem Statement and Significance for Information Systems}
\label{sec:problem-statement}

\subsection{The Information and Decision Problem}

The problem addressed by this project is the difficulty of maintaining a consistent, traceable connection between time-dependent business impacts, recovery requirements, and service recovery priorities. A list of critical services alone does not explain when their interruption becomes unacceptable, why a particular recovery target has been selected, or which supporting resources must be available for recovery. NIST's updated BIA guidance emphasizes centralized, reliable asset information and the relationship between asset criticality and enterprise objectives \cite{quinn2025bia}. Building on this concern, Masar focuses on the information and decision logic needed to interpret recovery priorities within a common environment.

The first aspect of the problem concerns the derivation of recovery requirements. An assessment may record impact severity at several elapsed-time intervals while leaving the connection between those observations and the resulting MTPD or RTO implicit. A category such as ``high impact'' does not establish an unacceptable consequence unless the organization has explicitly defined it that way. Furthermore, observations at discrete intervals may indicate a range within which a threshold is crossed, rather than an exact crossing time. A consistent approach therefore requires defined impact categories, time units, thresholds, and review responsibilities. It must preserve the distinction between the limit suggested by business consequences and a recovery target assessed against operational capability.

The second aspect concerns dependencies. Service criticality expresses business importance, whereas recovery precedence reflects the conditions required for resumption. A high-priority service may depend on a shared application or another service that must first become available. NIST identifies dependencies and resource recovery priorities as elements of contingency planning \cite{swanson2010contingency}. For Masar, this creates a requirement to represent service urgency together with its prerequisites. A ranked service list cannot, on its own, establish a feasible recovery schedule when restoration durations, staff availability, and other resource constraints have not been considered.

The third aspect concerns the integrity of the information supporting each result. A displayed priority may be misleading if it refers to an obsolete impact assessment, an unapproved target, or an incomplete dependency record. Centralization must consequently preserve ownership, assessment versions, rule configurations, and approval status, alongside the substantive continuity data. Missing evidence or inconsistent requirements should remain visible as conditions requiring review. Treating an absent assessment as a low impact would conceal uncertainty and undermine the interpretation of the resulting priority.

Together, these issues define the project's guiding question: \emph{how can an integrated, rule-based business continuity information system connect time-dependent impact assessments with reviewable recovery requirements and dependency-aware service priorities, while preserving the evidence supporting each result?} The question establishes an applied design problem. It calls for a method and an information structure that can be examined and evaluated, rather than presuming that a dashboard or a calculated ranking will necessarily improve recovery performance.

\subsection{Significance for Information Systems}

The problem is significant for information systems (IS) because it connects organizational priorities with the representation, processing, and use of information. J\"arvel\"ainen examines continuity management in the IS context and the relationship between IT incidents and business impacts \cite{jarvelainen2013incidents}. Masar addresses a related practical concern: enabling business and technical users to interpret recovery requirements through shared records and explicit decision logic. Its IS contribution lies in aligning data structures, user responsibilities, rules, and information presentation around the same recovery decisions.

The proposed integration provides a basis for tracing a displayed priority back to its service, impact assessment, recovery requirement, and applicable rule. It also distinguishes a calculated candidate from an approved requirement, allowing authorized personnel to review the result and retain a justification for any departure from it. The repository, method, and dashboard thus form complementary parts of the proposed artifact: the repository maintains the evidence, TDRP applies the decision logic, and the dashboard makes the outputs and their basis accessible.

This orientation is consistent with design science, which relates artifact construction and evaluation to relevant organizational problems \cite{hevner2004design}. The intended contribution is a documented method embodied in a usable information system for continuity decision support. TDRP is the working name of that proposed method; its contribution relative to existing approaches will be assessed through the literature review. The project does not rely on a claim that time-dependent impact analysis or dependency-aware prioritization is itself new.

\section{Importance of the Problem: The Broader Perspective}
\label{sec:importance}

Recovery priorities translate organizational obligations into choices about which services receive attention while disruption continues. The significance of those choices extends beyond technical restoration to the organization's ability to sustain its activities and responsibilities. Gibb and Buchanan's information-strategy perspective places continuity arrangements within this wider management context \cite{gibb2006framework}. From that perspective, recovery information must communicate business needs in a form that technical personnel can examine and act upon.

At the planning level, an explicit connection between impact assessments and recovery requirements can help service owners and technical teams examine the same assumptions. Service owners can review when consequences become unacceptable, while technical teams assess the resources and time needed to meet the proposed targets. At the response level, access to those requirements and their dependencies can support interpretation of priorities without requiring users to reconstruct the reasoning from disconnected records. These are the anticipated applications of Masar and the basis for investigating its practical value.

The proposed contribution also concerns accountability. The ability to inspect the source of a target or priority can support review when organizational needs, service dependencies, or recovery arrangements change. However, transparent rules do not compensate for incomplete assessments or unrealistic assumptions. Masar's usefulness will depend on the quality of its records, the appropriateness of its configured rules, and the responsibilities assigned to their review. Improvements in consistency, information-retrieval effort, and user task performance therefore remain outcomes for evaluation, rather than established benefits.

\section{Objectives and Proposed Solution}
\label{sec:objectives-solution}

\subsection{Project Objectives}
\label{sec:objectives}

The overall aim is to propose Masar as an integrated business continuity decision support system that connects time-dependent impact information, recovery requirements, and dependency-aware service priorities through the TDRP method. The first objective is to define the information and decision requirements needed to represent services, impact profiles, recovery targets, and dependencies consistently. The second objective is to formulate a transparent rule-based method for deriving candidate recovery requirements and relative priorities, with explicit treatment of incomplete or conflicting information. The third objective is to specify how a shared repository and dashboard will provide access to calculated results, supporting evidence, and approval states. The fourth objective is to establish an evaluation plan for the IS597 implementation that addresses rule consistency, output traceability, dependency visibility, and user task performance.

\subsection{Proposed System and TDRP Method}

Masar will organize continuity information around identifiable business services and their supporting resources. Service records will link to owners, impact assessments, recovery requirements, dependencies, and relevant procedures. Assessment records will identify the impact dimension, elapsed-time interval, severity category, and assessment version. This structure will provide the inputs for TDRP while preserving the context in which a requirement or priority was produced.

TDRP will apply an organization-defined impact matrix and explicit decision rules to the recorded impact profiles. Here, \emph{time-dependent} refers to the variation of assessed consequences with interruption duration; it does not imply live incident monitoring or automatic prediction. The method will identify the earliest assessed interval in which an approved unacceptability threshold is reached and use that evidence to inform a candidate MTPD. Where the assessment establishes only a threshold-crossing interval, the output will preserve that uncertainty. If the threshold is not reached within the assessment horizon, or relevant observations are missing, the available evidence will be marked for review rather than converted into an unsupported limit.

Candidate RTOs will be considered in relation to the tolerable disruption period and the activities needed to resume the required service level. Their review must account for supporting resources and recovery capability, including sufficient time for service resumption before consequences become unacceptable. Calculated candidates and approved targets will remain distinct. RPO and MBCO can be retained as contextual requirements, but the method will not infer acceptable data loss or minimum service capacity from interruption severity alone. Exact time bands, severity scales, and target-selection rules will be justified through the literature and detailed requirements work.

TDRP will prioritize services by relating their recovery requirements and criticality to documented dependencies. The result will present relative service priorities alongside prerequisites and unresolved conditions that affect their interpretation. Detailed design must address equal priorities, missing prerequisites, circular dependencies, and inconsistent target relationships. This provides a bounded decision support function: a reviewable ranking with dependency information, rather than an optimized allocation of personnel, equipment, or budgets.

The dashboard will present priorities, recovery requirements, dependencies, and exceptions, with access to their supporting records and rule explanations. An explicit rule-based approach is selected because its logic can be documented, inspected, and reproduced for the same inputs and configuration. Authorized personnel will retain responsibility for approving requirements and operational decisions, and the proposed workflow will record justified departures from calculated priorities. The system will display the method's results and associated information without executing recovery actions or generating autonomous recovery recommendations.

\subsection{Study Approach and Intended Evaluation}

Design science will organize the study across its academic and applied stages. Peffers et al. describe a process connecting problem identification and solution objectives with design, demonstration, evaluation, and communication \cite{peffers2007dsrm}. During IS595, this approach will guide the problem definition, literature synthesis, conceptual proposal, and discussion of anticipated applications. IS597 will continue with the detailed design, implementation, and evaluation of the proposed artifact.

Evaluation will address both the behavior of the rules and the usefulness of their presentation. Documented synthetic cases will allow comparison between expected results and system outputs, including threshold boundaries, incomplete assessments, equal priorities, and dependency conflicts. Traceability checks will examine whether each result can be linked to its assessment, applicable rules, and approval status. Subject to participant access and relevant institutional requirements, practitioner review and structured user tasks will assess whether the explanations and dependency information support the intended work.

A comparison with a manual workflow, where feasible, will use equivalent cases and information and examine task correctness, completion, and retrieval time. These measures will assess support for defined continuity tasks; they will not establish reductions in real incident losses or organizational downtime. The IS595 report will develop the proposal through a literature review organized from general BCM foundations to methods and systems most closely related to Masar, followed by discussion and anticipated applications, and a conclusion with future work. This sequence will establish the basis for the subsequent implementation without presenting anticipated outcomes as completed findings.
