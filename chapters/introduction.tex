\chapter{Introduction}
\label{ch:introduction}

\section{Motivation and Scope}
\label{sec:motivation-scope}

Business continuity is concerned with sustaining the services on which an organization depends when normal operations are disrupted. Restoring an application may be insufficient if the people, information, or supporting services needed to use it remain unavailable. Gibb and Buchanan position business continuity management (BCM) within an organization's information strategy, linking continuity arrangements to the management of operational change and disruption \cite{gibb2006framework}. This perspective motivates a service-oriented approach in which recovery requirements are connected to the resources and responsibilities that make service delivery possible.

Business impact analysis (BIA) provides an analytical basis for identifying recovery needs. It examines the consequences of interruption and informs judgments about which activities require priority. Torabi et al. explicitly address the identification of critical functions and continuity measures, including the maximum tolerable period of disruption (MTPD) and the minimum business continuity objective (MBCO) \cite{torabi2014framework}. In this project, MTPD denotes the point beyond which interruption of a specified service produces unacceptable consequences, whereas MBCO describes the minimum service level required during disruption.

Recovery requirements also have distinct time and data dimensions. NIST contingency planning guidance distinguishes tolerable business-process downtime, the recovery time objective (RTO) for system resources, and the recovery point objective (RPO) for data \cite{swanson2010contingency}. In Masar, RTO denotes the approved target duration for restoring a specified service or supporting resource to its required level; RPO concerns the acceptable recovery point for its data. The project records the scope of each requirement, distinguishing a service from its supporting system. An RTO is a target to be assessed against recovery capability, not evidence that the required restoration time can already be achieved.

Consider a continuity team reviewing a service whose interruption becomes more serious as downtime increases. The team needs to locate the relevant impact assessment, establish which recovery target is approved, identify supporting systems, and examine the reasoning behind the service's priority. If these records are maintained separately, the team must reconcile their identifiers, versions, and assumptions before using them together. This is the information-management situation addressed by the project; its prevalence or cost in a particular organization has not yet been established through fieldwork.

Masar is proposed as an integrated web-based decision support system incorporating a rule-based method named \emph{Time-Dependent Recovery Prioritization} (TDRP). TDRP will connect impact assessments over defined time intervals with candidate recovery requirements and relative recovery priorities. A shared repository and dashboard will expose the supporting records and rule outcomes. The intended users are continuity coordinators, business-service owners, and technical recovery personnel within a single organizational setting.

The current Research Seminar (IS595) establishes the problem, literature foundation, and conceptual proposal. Implementation, testing, and evaluation belong to the subsequent Project (IS597). The proposed scope covers service records, time-dependent impact assessments, recovery requirements, service-to-service and service-to-system dependencies, associated recovery procedures, and the presentation of calculated priorities. Automated recovery execution, predictive incident detection, machine learning, disruption simulation, and optimization of resource-constrained recovery schedules are outside this scope. RPO and MBCO may be retained as contextual requirements, but TDRP will not infer either value from interruption severity alone.

\section{Problem Statement and Significance for Information Systems}
\label{sec:problem-statement}

\subsection{The Information and Decision Problem}

The problem addressed by this project is how to maintain a traceable connection between business impact information, recovery requirements, and recovery priorities within one decision support environment. A list of critical services does not, by itself, establish when their interruption becomes unacceptable, which version of a target has been approved, or which supporting resources constrain recovery. NIST's updated guidance on BIA emphasizes the value of centralized, reliable asset information and of linking asset criticality to enterprise objectives \cite{quinn2025bia}. Masar adopts this information-management concern as the starting point for its proposed artifact.

Three related difficulties define the problem. First, an impact assessment can describe consequences at several elapsed-time intervals without providing a transparent record of how those observations led to an MTPD or an RTO. A label such as ``high impact'' is insufficient unless the organization has defined what that category means and whether it represents an unacceptable consequence. Different impact dimensions may also cross their thresholds at different times. A reproducible method therefore needs explicit categories, time units, decision rules, and approval responsibilities. Where the available observations establish only an interval in which a threshold is crossed, presenting an exact crossing time would imply precision that the assessment does not support.

Second, relative service criticality and technical recovery precedence are different judgments. A high-priority service may depend on a shared system that must be restored before that service can operate. Conversely, recovering an important system does not necessarily restore every business activity that uses it. NIST's contingency planning guidance identifies dependencies and resource recovery priorities as inputs to recovery planning \cite{swanson2010contingency}. For Masar, this implies a need to display service urgency alongside prerequisite relationships. A ranked service list should not be interpreted as a complete executable recovery schedule, especially when recovery durations and resource constraints are not modeled.

Third, the underlying records must remain interpretable when they are brought together. A priority associated with an obsolete assessment, an unapproved target, or an incomplete dependency record may appear authoritative without being dependable. Masar therefore needs to retain the relationship between each output and its input assessment, rule configuration, ownership, and approval state. Centralization is useful only when it preserves these meanings and makes missing or conflicting information visible. The project will treat incomplete evidence as an exception requiring review, rather than silently assigning a low impact or a convenient recovery target.

The proposed design problem can consequently be stated as follows: \emph{how can an integrated, rule-based business continuity information system connect time-dependent impact assessments with reviewable recovery requirements and dependency-aware recovery priorities, while retaining the evidence and human accountability associated with each output?} This question guides the requirements of Masar. It does not presuppose that a rule-based system will outperform existing practice or that an organization has already experienced measurable delays. Such claims require subsequent evaluation.

\subsection{Significance for Information Systems}

The problem sits at the intersection of organizational decision-making and the design of information systems (IS). J\"arvel\"ainen examines continuity management in the IS context and the relationship between IT incidents and business impacts \cite{jarvelainen2013incidents}. Masar focuses on a related applied issue: representing business requirements and technical dependencies in a form that supports consistent interpretation by different users. Its IS significance lies in the alignment of data structures, decision rules, user responsibilities, and information presentation.

A dashboard alone would leave the derivation of priorities unresolved; a rule set alone would leave ownership, record consistency, and access to supporting information unresolved. The proposed contribution is their integration around a defined recovery-information workflow. A user should be able to move from a displayed priority to the relevant service, assessment interval, recovery requirement, and rule explanation. Authorized personnel should also be able to distinguish a calculated candidate from an approved requirement and record a justified departure from a calculated priority. These are proposed design requirements, not features already implemented or validated.

Design science provides a suitable orientation because it relates the construction and evaluation of an artifact to a relevant organizational problem \cite{hevner2004design}. For this non-thesis master's project, the intended contribution is a documented and evaluable method embodied in an information system. The name TDRP identifies the project's proposed method; it is not a claim that time-dependent impact analysis or dependency-aware prioritization is new. The extent to which this particular integration differs from previous work must be established in the literature review.

\section{Importance of the Problem: The Broader Perspective}
\label{sec:importance}

The wider importance of the problem follows from the relationship between service continuity and organizational obligations. Recovery priorities express choices about which services receive attention while disruption continues. Those choices should be defensible in terms of the consequences of interruption, the conditions required for service resumption, and the organization's accepted limits. BCM is therefore also a management and coordination concern, consistent with the information-strategy perspective advanced by Gibb and Buchanan \cite{gibb2006framework}.

At the planning level, a traceable link between impact assessments and recovery requirements can make disagreements easier to examine. Business owners can review whether impact categories represent unacceptable consequences, while technical personnel can assess whether a proposed target is feasible. At the disruption-response level, a consolidated view can make the relationship between urgency and prerequisites more visible. These are anticipated uses of Masar. Reduced information-retrieval effort, improved consistency, or faster decisions remain potential benefits to investigate rather than established outcomes.

The distinction matters because a transparent calculation can still be based on incomplete assessments or unrealistic targets. The proposed system must therefore expose uncertainty and approval status as part of its outputs. It must also avoid implying that a service's high priority guarantees immediate recovery. By keeping these qualifications visible, Masar aims to support accountable discussion between business and technical users. Its value will depend on the quality of the underlying information and the suitability of the configured rules, as well as on the software that presents them.

\section{Objectives and Proposed Solution}
\label{sec:objectives-solution}

\subsection{Project Objectives}
\label{sec:objectives}

The overall goal is to establish an academically grounded proposal for Masar, a web-based business continuity decision support system integrating the TDRP method with shared continuity information. The first objective is to define the information and decision requirements connecting service impact assessments, recovery targets, and dependencies. The second objective is to formulate a transparent rule-based approach for identifying candidate recovery requirements and relative priorities, including how incomplete evidence and conflicting requirements should be handled. The third objective is to specify how a shared repository and dashboard will make the inputs, applied rules, and approval states accessible to continuity stakeholders. The fourth objective is to establish a feasible evaluation plan for the IS597 implementation, covering rule consistency, output traceability, dependency visibility, and user task performance.

\subsection{Conceptual Operation of Masar}

Masar will organize continuity information around identifiable business services and their supporting resources. Each service record is expected to link to its owner, impact assessment, approved recovery requirements, dependencies, and relevant recovery procedures. Assessment records will distinguish the impact dimension, elapsed-time interval, severity category, and assessment version. This structure is intended to prevent results calculated from one assessment or rule version from being mistaken for those generated from another.

TDRP will use an organization-defined impact matrix and explicit decision rules. At a conceptual level, the method will identify the earliest assessed interval in which a defined impact dimension meets an approved unacceptability threshold. That result will inform a candidate MTPD for review. If the threshold is not reached within the assessment horizon, the result will indicate that the available horizon does not establish the limit. If assessments are missing or contradictory, the result will require review. A generic high-impact label will be treated as an unacceptability threshold only where that interpretation has been explicitly approved.

Candidate RTOs will be considered within the tolerable disruption period, allowing for the activities needed to resume the specified service level. A time interval will not automatically determine a feasible technical target: recovery capability, prerequisites, and business approval must also be considered. The rules will distinguish calculated candidates from approved targets and identify cases in which the proposed recovery arrangement cannot meet the required time. Exact time bands, severity scales, tie-breaking rules, and approval procedures remain design decisions to be justified through the literature and subsequent requirements work.

For prioritization, TDRP will relate approved recovery requirements and service criticality to recorded dependencies. Its output will present relative service priorities together with the prerequisites that may affect their interpretation. Missing prerequisites, circular dependencies, and inconsistent target relationships will need explicit treatment during detailed design. The initial method will not claim to find an optimal recovery sequence or to allocate personnel, equipment, or budgets. It will provide a reviewable prioritization result and the information needed to understand it.

The dashboard will present service priorities, recovery requirements, dependency information, and exceptions with access to the supporting assessments and rule explanations. It will display calculated results and associated records; it will not generate autonomous recovery recommendations, execute recovery procedures, or replace incident command. Where authorized users depart from a calculated priority, the proposed workflow will retain their justification. An explicit rule-based approach is selected to make this reasoning inspectable and reproducible under the same inputs and configuration. Whether the resulting rules are appropriate remains an evaluation question.

\subsection{Study Approach and Intended Evaluation}

The project will use design science as an organizing approach. Peffers et al. describe a process linking problem identification and solution objectives to design, demonstration, evaluation, and communication \cite{peffers2007dsrm}. During IS595, this orientation supports the problem definition, literature synthesis, proposed method, and discussion of anticipated applications. During IS597, it will guide implementation and assessment of the resulting artifact. The current chapter reports no implementation results, participant findings, or demonstrated operational improvements.

The intended evaluation will examine whether the implementation follows its documented rules, whether users can trace outputs to their evidence, and whether dependencies and unresolved conditions are clearly exposed. Synthetic, documented cases will support checks of threshold boundaries, absent data, equal priorities, and dependency conflicts. Subject to access and relevant institutional requirements, practitioner review and structured user tasks may assess the usefulness of explanations and the effort required to retrieve relevant information. If a manual-workflow comparison is feasible, it will use equivalent cases and information, reporting task completion, correctness, and retrieval time. These measures assess the artifact's support for defined tasks; they do not establish reductions in real incident losses or improvements in organizational resilience.

The intended IS595 report will proceed from this introduction to a literature review organized from general BCM foundations to the specific methods and systems relevant to Masar. A discussion of anticipated applications will then develop the implications and boundaries of the proposal, followed by a conclusion and future work. Detailed architecture, implementation, testing, and evaluation will be addressed in the IS597 continuation.
